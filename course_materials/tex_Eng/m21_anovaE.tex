\section{Comparison of Means and Model Comparison}

Since the second half of the term, the use of probability has changed to focus on the population. Previously, probability was introduced on the basis that errors followed a normal distribution. However, the approach to errors that are affected by various factors has come from another aspect of normal distribution. Additionally, by distinguishing between population and sample, the distribution of the population can be assumed, and the distribution of the sample statistic can be considered, which is used to estimate the parameters. Not only do we estimate the parameters, but we also explain the process of conducting practical judgment through the null hypothesis test.

\input{m21_anova_escape_02}

When we first talked about hypothesis testing, we said that it is a model comparison ($\to$ Section \ref{ModelComparison1}, Pp. \pageref{ModelComparison1}, and Section \ref{ModelComparison2}, Pp. \pageref{ModelComparison2}). Hypothesis testing is the process of comparing a null hypothesis and an alternative hypothesis to determine which one is more likely. It's like making a decision about which model is the winner.

The word "model" here may give a different impression than the expression of the previous (linear) model, since it includes phrases such as assuming the null hypothesis to be $\mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0. However, this is also a model, and moreover, a linear model. I will now explain how to conceptually integrate "hypothesis testing" and "linear models".

As an example, we prepared the following data (Figure \ref{fig::21_01}). This is a plot of the average values of two groups (let's assume they are an experimental group and a control group) without correspondence. The average values shown here are sample means, so as you can see, there is a difference. By the way, the black dots are the observed values ​​of each individual in each group.

\subsection{Experimental Plan from the Perspective of God}
Let's look at the same data from a different perspective here.

In this example, we do not know the characteristics of psychological variables, which are dependent variables, because they are not visible. However, considering that mental issues are the result of countless factors, assuming a normal distribution is natural. In other words, both the experimental group and the control group are samples from the same normal population, which can be viewed as the same humans from a larger perspective.

The participants were randomly assigned to the experimental and control groups by the experimenter in a randomized controlled trial (RCT). If there were no individual differences, we could simply compare the scores of one person from each group and consider it. However, since we are dealing with humans, individual differences cannot be avoided. Therefore, we consider gathering multiple people to offset individual differences. We will look at the difference in the means of each group to see the effect of the manipulation.

If there is no effect in this experiment, the average values of the two groups will be the same. In this case, since there is no difference between the treatment group and control group, the overall average should be the sum of the scores of both groups. No effect means the overall average. However, if you look at each group separately, there is a difference from the overall average. As shown in Figure 21.03, the control group has a relatively low average, while the treatment group has a relatively high average. This difference is the effect of the experiment, indicated by the short green arrow in Figure 21.03.

Now, the average for each group can be written as follows:
\[ \bar{X}_1 =\frac{1}{5} \sum_{i=1}^5 X_{i1} \]
\[ \bar{X}_2 =\frac{1}{5} \sum_{i=1}^5 X_{i2} \]
In addition, the grand mean can be written as follows: \footnote{"gm" is an abbreviation for "grand mean."}
\[ \bar{X}_{gm} = \frac{1}{10} \sum_{i} \sum_{j} X_{ij} \]
This is the sample mean, but what we really want to discuss is the (hypothetical) population mean without any differences between groups. While $\bar{X}_{gm}$ can be used as an estimate for the population mean, the great thing about models is that we can idealize and abstract, so let's use the Greek letter $\beta$ as an unknown parameter for the overall mean and denote it as $\beta_0$.

Next, let's call the effect of groupings $\beta_1$. Then, the model for the average of each group is as follows:
\[ \mu_1= \beta_0 + \beta_1 \]
\[ \mu_2 = \beta_0  -\beta_1 \]
This can be expressed as "θ", as the left-hand side also denotes the mean of each group, and is represented by a Greek letter that signifies an unknown variable.

Next, individual differences here are errors that cannot be controlled in this experiment, and it is unknown in which direction they will affect the group average. To express this, it can be said that...
\[ X_{ij} = \mu_j + \varepsilon_{ij} \]
This means that you are a translator.
\[ X_{i1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 + \varepsilon_{i1} \]
\[ X_{i2} = \beta_0 - \beta_1 + \varepsilon_{i2} \]
You can write it as follows. Here's a trick: the plus and minus signs attached to $\beta_1$ can be thought of as multiplying by $+1$ or $-1$. If we express what is determining these signs as $X_{1}=+1,X_{2}=-1$, we can write it all together as follows.

Do you recognize this text from somewhere? Yes, it has the same form as the regression analysis we did earlier (refer to section \ref{m09_Regression}).

Let's briefly review regression analysis here. The idea is that when we have two variables, $x_i$ and $y_i$, we assume that there is some functional relationship between them. Since a functional relationship means that one variable determines the other, we can predict one variable if we know the other by expressing this relationship as a mathematical equation. So, what is the simplest functional relationship? It is the linear function $y=ax+b$ which we learned in middle school. However, unlike in middle school, we now have a lot of data, and we cannot draw a line that fits perfectly to all of the data, so we need to think about drawing a line that fits most of the data with some margin of error. This line is called the regression line or the best fit line. If we denote the predicted value by this function (linear function or linear model) as $\hat{y}_i$, then...

\[ \hat{y}_i = b_0 + b_1x_i \]
Because data $y_i$ is accompanied by an error $e_i$ when appearing with a predicted value $\hat{y}_i$,
\[ y_i = \hat{y}_i + e_i  \]
So, to integrate this,
\[ y_i = b_0 + b_1 x_i + e_i \]
The intention was to do so.

Well well. Although the symbols have changed from $y_i, x_i$ and $b_0, b_1$ to $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1", the basic form is still a linear function of the form $y=ax+b$ (see Figure \ref{fig::21_05}).

Earlier, I said that "no effect" means the overall average. If we represent the effect with the symbol $\beta_1$, then "no effect" means $\beta_1=0$. In that case, the term $\beta_1X_j$ disappears. This means that the data comes from a single normal distribution, with an overall average of $\beta_0$ and added errors $\varepsilon_{ij}$.

One common normal distribution. Two groups with the same normal distribution that cannot be separated. It's about time we connected this to the null hypothesis test. Saying there is no effect is exactly the null hypothesis. In other words, the null hypothesis is a linear model with a flat line crossing the group means, where the slope is zero and for figure \ref{fig::21_03} it's $\beta_1=0$. Testing for the difference in mean values is the logic for concluding that the group means are not on a flat line, i.e. the slope isn't zero, and the alternative model being compared is some "non-zero slope" model.
\input{m21_anova_escape_06}
The null hypothesis assumes that the slope is zero, while the alternative hypothesis is a loose model that states that anything other than zero is acceptable. Therefore, even if the null hypothesis is rejected, we can only claim that there "might be some difference," but we cannot strongly argue that "if not zero, then what?" Hypothesis testing should be used as a supplement, and it is important to consider the substantive size of the difference, such as how much it is leaning, what the confidence interval of the difference is, and how large the effect size (standardized mean difference) is.

During the previous regression analysis ($\to$ Chapter \ref{m09_Regression}, Section \ref{LeastSquare}, Pp.\pageref{LeastSquare}), we used the least squares method to calculate regression coefficients. However, at that time we did not introduce statistical inference. We simply explained that we could calculate the coefficients that best fit the data. We only talked about sample data, so we wrote the coefficients as $b_0$ and $b_1$. However, if we consider this within the framework of statistical inference, what we want to know is the slope in the population, which is a population parameter. Therefore, this time we will use Greek letters such as $\beta_1$ and $\varepsilon_{ij}$ to represent the coefficients.

I've been talking about how hypothesis testing for differences in means and regression analysis are essentially the same thing. Both rely on the assumption of normal distribution, and while their origins may differ subtly in terms of whether they represent the distribution of "overall trends assumed for psychological phenomena" or "error distribution," mathematically speaking they are the same thing. Therefore, regardless of which approach you take, you can use them in the same way.

Some people may wonder whether we use the least squares method to estimate the mean within the framework of inferential statistics, just as we obtain the coefficients of a regression analysis using the least squares method. Actually, this is an extremely interesting point. However, because the framework of inferential statistics involves using a probability distribution called the normal distribution, we need to use a different approach from the analysis that aims to minimize errors analytically. We will explain two methods for estimating parameters using a probability distribution: the Maximum Likelihood method and the Bayesian method. However, to cut to the chase, the good news is that the answers obtained using the least squares method and the Maximum Likelihood method are \textbf{exactly the same}.

By the way, the methods for estimating population parameters from sample mean and sample variance that have been used so far are classified as "Moment method". In any case, if the data is the same, the estimated values will be roughly the same. Since we are trying to estimate the same thing, it would be troublesome if the estimates are different.

\section{One-Way Between-Subjects ANOVA Test}
So far, we have talked about comparing the averages of two levels, but what happens when we have three or more levels?
This can be thought of as an extension of the linear model framework, in essence. For example, suppose we have data like that shown in Table \ref{table::21_02}. There is one factor with three levels, making this an example of a between-group design.
\input{m21_anova_escape_07}
The mean of each group is $\bar{X}_1 = 5.5,\bar{X}_2 = 6.0,\bar{X}_3 = 4.25$, and the overall mean is $\bar{X}_{gm}= 5.25$.
In the context of hypothesis testing, the null hypothesis becomes the hypothesis that "there is no difference in the population mean," so symbolically, it can be expressed as $\mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu_3$ .
In terms of linear models, as shown in Figure \ref{fig::21_06}, a linear model without slope, i.e., a horizontal line of $X_{ij} = \bar{X}_{gm} + \varepsilon_{ij}$, can be considered.

\input{m21_anova_escape_08}

The alternative hypothesis negates the null hypothesis, implying there is a difference somewhere in the population mean or there is a slope (i.e. not a straight line with no slope). However, note that when we say "not a straight line," we cannot just deduce whether it is an upward sloping or downward sloping line because the levels 1/2/3 on the horizontal axis are just nominal level measures. It means that the reason why we chose 1 as the control group while assigning 2 or 3 to "Group A" or "Group B" is not significant, and any other arrangement would be equally acceptable. In fact, nominal level measures require that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the object and the numbers used, but it does not imply any order significance for the numbers. In Figure \ref{fig::21_06}, we can see that the trend is rising toward level 2 but declining from level 2 to level 3, and it cannot be represented by a single straight line. However, if we change the order to level 3/1/2, we can assign an upward sloping line (see Figure \ref{fig::21_07}). Of course, we could also arrange the order to be 2/1/3 and apply a downward sloping line. Either way, the information contained in the data is the same. Therefore, let us limit the alternative hypothesis to "not a straight line" and "population mean is not the same."

\input{m21_anova_escape_09}

\input{m21_anova_escape_10}


The analysis method that adds elements of the null hypothesis test to the factor planning is called \keytermE{Analysis of Variance}{ANOVA}. Sometimes it is abbreviated as ANOVA. Although it is called "variance" analysis, please note that what is being tested is the "difference in mean values". The reason why it is called variance analysis is related to the fact that the slope of the line cannot be simply determined to be either upward or downward. Because it can vary positively or negatively, the calculation is squared to eliminate sign effects.

During the first term, we calculated the magnitude of the effect by squaring it, but we left out how to compare the effect with the error.
The reason for this is that without the framework of inferential statistics, we cannot refer to methods of comparison, and without the framework of null hypothesis testing, we cannot refer to criteria for judgment. However, we can now explain using these frameworks. We can consider the distribution of the sum of squares, that is, the squared and combined values, and calculate the statistical quantity from the theoretical distribution. Let's take a closer look at this method.

Now, when the result of the analysis of variance is judged to be "significant," meaning the null hypothesis has been rejected and the alternative hypothesis accepted, we may wonder which of the multiple alternative hypotheses that were considered is actually valid. However, we cannot determine this. All we know is that the null hypothesis is false.

So, what should we do? One easy solution would be to systematically test the various alternative sub-models suggested above. For example, we could conduct three independent t-tests for each pair of groups (i.e. Group 1 vs Group 2, Group 1 vs Group 3, Group 2 vs Group 3) without matching their observations. Some people may think this approach is sufficient and stop here. However, as the number of levels increases, so does the number of possible pairwise comparisons: six pairs for four levels (1-2, 1-3, 1-4, 2-3, 2-4, 3-4) and ten pairs for five levels (1-2, 1-3, 1-4, 1-5, 2-3, 2-4, 2-5, 3-4, 3-5, 4-5). Although the workload may increase as the number of levels increases, we should persevere knowing that the computer will do the calculations for us.

However, this approach does not work well as it is. As we learned in Section \ref{alphaInfration} on page \pageref{alphaInfration} in the previous Lecture \ref{m19_NHST2}, repeated testing increases the risk of Type I errors.
The reason for this is that the null hypothesis test involves making a probabilistic judgement, which means that when there is a significant difference, there is an error (type 1 error) at the significance level of alpha. If this probabilistic judgement is repeated, it can actually cause problems.

Let's consider an analogy. Suppose there is a skilled stockbroker who has a good eye for the market and the stocks he chooses usually go up in value. Of course, he may occasionally make mistakes, let's assume that the probability of failure is 1 in 20 times. That means a failure rate of 5%. Assuming that customers who invest with him have a 95% chance of making a profit, the broker can make a profit for his clients in 95% of cases. Now, suppose he receives a request from a group of 10 people. If he needs to introduce each of them to a different investment opportunity, can he use his 95% win rate to ensure that all of them make a profit this time around?

% The 5% failure rate (95% success rate) mentioned in this context refers to the issue of Type 1 errors, where there is a risk of rejecting the null hypothesis (no difference) even though it is actually true. The fact that there are 10 requests this time can be thought of as conducting tests for each pair, similar to a 5-level factor plan.
The probability of making a correct judgment in one test is 95\%, but the probability of making two consecutive correct judgments is $0.95\times0.95=0.9025$. This means that the alpha level has increased to approximately 10\% ($1-0.9025=0.0975$). Similarly, in a group of 10 people, there is a probability of approximately 40\% ($1-0.95^{10}=0.4013$) that there will be at least one incorrect judgment.

%Even with a 5% chance of danger and 95% accuracy, since it is a probabilistic judgment, repeating it can result in unexpectedly large numbers. Therefore, it is not appropriate to think that it is fine to repeat t-tests from the beginning even if there are many levels, and it is also not good to repeat t-tests when adding additional tests after conducting analysis of variance and achieving significance.

So, what should we do next? Following the analysis of variance, we have what is called a \keytermE{post-hoc analysis}{下位検定 (かいけんてい)} or simply a \keytermE{post-hoc analysis}{事後検定 (じごけんてい)}. Various methods for conducting such an analysis have been proposed, and it's hard to say which is good or bad (there are entire books dedicated to this topic alone \citep{BA33892274}). However, to help you out, I'll introduce two methods.

\paragraph{Bonferroni's Method} The problem at hand is essentially an inflation of the $\alpha$ level, so one solution is to adjust it downwards. For instance, with three alternative hypotheses and a desire to maintain a 5\% significance level overall, one could judge each hypothesis at the level of $0.05/3=0.017$. This is an intuitive and transparent criterion. There are also various improved versions of this approach, such as the Holm method, Shaffer's procedure, and Holland-Copenhaver's method.

\paragraph{Tukey's HSD method} This is the most common and widely used method\footnote{However, "commonly used" does not necessarily mean "correct" or "superior." Each method has different assumptions, premises, areas of focus, and emphases, and "commonly used" often only means that statistical software packages frequently calculate this statistic.}. In this method, a different statistical value $q$ is calculated for each pair, and it is determined whether this value clears the reference line.

In addition, there are various methods and options available through statistical packages. Therefore, it is important to accurately record and report "which analysis method was used and how the judgments were made."

% Above all, it is important to remember that these judgments are all techniques for "probabilistically" determining the presence or absence of differences. Judgments of presence or absence reduce a lot of data or information into 1 bit. Simply reporting that there was a difference does not convey whether it was a slight or significant difference. Since these judgments are also probabilistic, there is a possibility of error. Depending on the method, even lower-level testing may or may not detect differences. Therefore, seeking a method in which differences will be detected is actually a misuse of scientific practice\footnote{This harmful practice is called p-hacking.}. Instead, it is important to directly evaluate the magnitude of the difference or the effect size, and consider the range in which the estimated parameters might fall, in terms of the practice of science.

% In this section, we have explained the problems with repeating statistical tests and their avoidance strategies. However, historically speaking, even among professional scholars in research practice, these issues have not always been given sufficient importance. Although we discussed an example of three levels of analysis in one analysis, if three experiments are conducted in a single paper and a t-test is performed for each, the same "inflation of $\alpha$ level" problem will arise. While confirming phenomena across multiple experiments is important, because probabilistic judgments are being made, the overall significant level must be planned systematically throughout\footnote{The $\alpha$ level per analysis is called family-wise $\alpha$, and the $\alpha$ level per paper is called experimental-wise $\alpha$.}. However, in reality, it is not uncommon for many t-tests to be repeated for one study or for no adjustment to be made for the $\alpha$ level across multiple experiments. Unfortunately, there is a reality that research has been accumulated in the past that has been "wrong". Unless each person takes this issue seriously, it will become a crisis for the industry as a whole.

The null hypothesis test requires a highly specialized and delicate technique to be properly understood and used; however, due to machines easily performing the calculations, incorrect use of the technique has become widespread. To counteract this, one must either acquire correct knowledge or simply avoid using the null hypothesis test. There are methods that do not require a one-bit judgment using the null hypothesis test. One method is to simply report the size, such as confidence intervals or effect size. Another is to use Bayesian estimation of probability models rather than estimation using moment methods. We may have an opportunity to discuss this latter topic in the latter half of this course.
