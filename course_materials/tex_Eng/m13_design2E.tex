Let's estimate the magnitude of the effect and the size of the error. The approach is the same even in the case of two factors.
As it was randomly assigned, all scattering of data without any human intervention should be attributed to errors (individual differences). Since the errors should cancel each other out, theoretically they should all coincide with the mean value.

However, since there are different interventions by individuals such as temperature operations and differences in manufacturers, and since it is a human action, individual differences have also been added. Therefore, I think that the data became scattered.

Assuming that all should be the same ideally, and without conducting any experiments or observing any individual differences, the ideal value of the data should perfectly match the overall mean, $\bar{X}_{gm} = 9.75$. However, the effect of temperature, $b_1$, comes into play. As shown on the left side of Figure \ref{fig::13_02}, let's calculate the average value of only the Hot group, after grouping by temperature.
\[ \bar{y}_{H} = \frac{1}{6}\sum_{i=1}^6 y_i = \frac{1}{6} (13+11+12+7+6+8)= 9.5 \]

\[ \bar{y}_{C} = \frac{1}{6}\sum_{i=7}^{12}y_i = \frac{1}{6}(9+9+9+13+11+9) = 10.0 \]

The hot coffee (-0.25) of stock A (+0.75) is priced at 10.25, and since the group average is 12.0, there is a difference of +1.75 (12.0-10.25). This is also one of the effects to be considered. This is the combination effect that occurs \textbf{only} when combining "A" and "hot", and is called an \keytermE{interaction}. (As a side note, in a statistical context, "interaction" is translated as "交互作用", while in a social psychology context, it is translated as "相互作用".)

Interaction refers to the special effect that arises from the combination of levels. In contrast to interaction, the average of the groups we have seen so far is referred to as the \keytermE{main effect}. The linear model mentioned in Equation \ref{eq13_03} must be updated to include the interaction term, as shown in Equation \ref{eq13_04}.

This interaction, as mentioned in Equation \ref{eq13_05}, is an independent source of influence separate from the main effects. Therefore, it is possible to have only interaction without main effects. For example, in this case, differences in temperature or brand may not be clear, but if brand B becomes incredibly delicious when warmed up, that would be an example of only interaction.

In other words, interaction is like a condition that says "but only if he's handsome". Even if cool lines or gestures don't necessarily increase one's evaluation (although they could be a bonus), they are especially good when done by handsome men. On the other hand, if someone who isn't handsome does them, it can have the opposite effect, so keep that in mind. The signs of the interaction term ($b_3$) alternate between plus and minus, following the sequence of +,-,-,+ from top to bottom. Let me add an explanation as to why this is the case.

As this is a $2 \times 2$ design, as shown in figure \ref{fig::13_03}, we can see that the main effects are based on the average values around that area. For example, with the first factor, which is temperature (hereafter referred to as factor A), the effect of "Hot (level a1)" or "Cold (level b1)" is calculated based on the average value of a group of columns obtained by collapsing the other factor, manufacturer, and represented by $b_1$. Since the comparison between these levels is relative, levels 1 and 2 are opposite in sign, represented by $+b_1$ and $-b_1$. Similarly, with the second factor, which is brand (hereafter referred to as factor B), the effect is calculated by collapsing the levels of factor A into a single group of rows, obtaining the average values, and represented by $+b_2$ and $-b_2$ for relative comparison.

In contrast, interaction is the effect occurring within the cells for each combination. The main effects for each factor are calculated in the row and column directions, thus the interactions need to be summed to zero in both directions, otherwise the overall calculation will not be correct. For example, if there is an interaction effect $b_3$ at level a1b1 in the top left of Figure \ref{fig::13_04}, we need to make sure that the sum for the same level a1 is not zero in the column direction, which means $-b_3$ should be added in the a1b2 level. Additionally, in order to ensure that the sum for the row direction is also not zero, a2b1 also needs to be $-b_3$. Then, for a2b2, in order to make the sum zero in both the row and column directions, we need to add $+b_3$. In this way, the sign of the interaction effect is determined in relation to the surroundings. The magnitude of the interaction is determined such that regardless of the row and column direction, the sum is always zero even if the number of levels is increased.
