\chapter{Fundamentals of Measurement}
\label{m06_ctt}

The purpose of this lecture, psychological statistics, is a discipline that aims to \textbf{"see, measure, and think about" the invisible}. In order to see the invisible, it is necessary to establish a way of thinking about and methodology for measurement. In this lecture, we will explain the ideas behind measurement and basic models.
\section{Testing and Psychology}
The mind, which is the subject of psychological research, cannot be seen or touched. In fact, we can't even confirm if it really exists. We believe that not just ourselves but also others and other human beings have a "mind," but unlike tangible objects, there is no way to confirm whether it truly exists\footnote{Even tangible objects that we see might not be really there. Kant, in his book "Critique of Pure Reason" \citep{Kant1961jo, Kant1962ge}, continued to consider what it means for things to be visible and exist. Those who are interested may want to read introductory books like \citet{Kurosaki2000}.}. In a way, we simply choose to believe that such a thing as a "mind" exists. Conversely, if we agree to its existence, we can also say that pets have a mind\footnote{We say that pets are part of the family, but since all family members behave as if the animal has a self, at least within that family it is considered that the pet has a social existence. However, for those who dislike animals, even a beloved pet may be seen as nothing more than a type of animal. There is also the fact that harming a pet is treated not as an act against persons but rather as a criminal offense for property destruction.}. Either way, the point here is that the existence of the mind is merely an assumption or "pretend." The mind is a model that represents its functional aspects.

The word "model" can refer to a scale model or an ideal form, but here it refers to an abstractly conceived mechanism or structure. The human mind is the model we have assumed to understand people. A model describes how it behaves, and if the description is mathematical, we call it a mathematical model. If it incorporates probability, we call it a probabilistic model, and if it describes the relationship between statistical indicators, we call it a statistical model. The description can be in natural language, but as we discussed in Chapter \ref{m01_introduction}, expressing relationships in mathematics is the basic style of modern science. By expressing things mathematically, we can achieve precision, abstraction, and generalization.

The fundamental model of psychology, or the research paradigm in psychology, is the \keytermE{SR model}, as shown in Figure \ref{fig::01_01} in Chapter \ref{m01_introduction}. This model suggests that within the black box of the human mind, there exists a model of the "mind" that is composed of combinations of stimuli and responses. Psychology is a field that has attempted to explore the various methods and approaches surrounding these pairs of stimuli and responses. On the other hand, there are also attempts to look at something that cannot be seen within this framework, such as through the familiar concept of "testing."

I believe that everyone has taken tests in various scenes before. Most of them were probably academic tests at school.
By the way, when you think about it again, what is "academic ability"? The Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology advocates the cultivation of various abilities in the guidelines for educational instruction, but while things like "learning ability" and "life skills" are clearly defined, they are not well understood.
Without a doubt, at least in educational testing, we are measuring academic ability. Academic ability is an invisible, inner strength within individuals, and tests designed to measure this have a deep connection with psychology.

Most academic tests are conducted using pen and paper. Various questions are prepared on the test paper and examinees answer them. Answers are classified into correct and incorrect, and a higher number of correct answers indicates higher academic ability, while a lower number indicates lower academic ability\footnote{In reality, scoring is not only based on the number of correct answers, but sometimes total scores are adjusted according to the importance of each question.}.
In other words, I believe that consistent knowledge, academic ability, and skills for accurately grasping and judging questions are maintained within an individual when presented with various stimuli and receiving a multitude of correct responses. The fact that the response patterns are the same suggests that the black box contains these attributes, which is the very essence of psychological models.

\subsection{Views on Personality}


For example, in the field of psychology, personality is often seen as "the stable behavioral tendencies that an individual possesses." It is important to recognize the existence of this "stable something," and if a person has a stable nature, it can be anticipated that they will respond with the same pattern of behaviors to various stimuli. Personality tests are designed for this purpose.

The field of personality psychology started with the idea of judging people based on their appearance, such as their physical build. For example, one might assume that someone who is muscular is serious and someone who is overweight is cheerful. One person, Gal, developed "osteognomony," which involves judging personality based on the shape of the skull. However, these ideas have been dismissed as biased and discriminatory. This approach is called typology, and while it simplifies things, there are exceptions that don't fit into these categories. Therefore, personality psychology has shifted from typology to trait theory. Trait theory posits that the characteristics that define a person's personality are common to all humans, but the degree to which they manifest varies between individuals. For example, some people are bright and cheery, while others are somber and serious. These differences can be understood as variations in the degree of "extraversion," or the extent to which a person is outgoing. Darker individuals have a lower score on the extraversion scale, while brighter individuals have a higher score \citep{Oshio2020}.

This is called a personality test, which can measure such characteristics. This can be considered as the personality version of the test theory. Unlike academic tests, there is not just one kind of invisible thing that we want to measure. For example, a Japanese language test measures Japanese language ability, and a math test measures math ability. In other words, what we want to measure is one dimension. On the other hand, personality is an individual's characteristic that is reflected in various behaviors, so the issue is how many dimensions it has. Based on the history and statistical analysis of personality tests, it has been concluded that it is better to measure personality in five dimensions. Please keep in mind that academic tests and personality tests are tools with the same purpose, but with different focuses.

Exams for lower grades in elementary school are often made for the latter purpose, and it is not uncommon to get a perfect score. Here, we will consider tests that aim to measure the level of academic ability in a more detailed way. Please note that this is different from so-called psychological tests such as "you chose a rabbit, so you are secretly timid," which are just for fun and have nothing to do with measuring anything. People who play such games often claim that they are based on statistics, but without scientific procedures and data that can be quantitatively evaluated and statistically analyzed, it is nothing but a nuisance and a hindrance to business.

Now, if we were to design a test with the aim of measuring invisible abilities for the purpose of character assessment, what would the ideal test look like? To make it easier to think about, let's consider the opposite of the ideal test, the worst possible test. Have you ever thought that the test you've taken was not a good one? What kind of test was it?

The title of the text is "Unfair Tests". It describes a situation where people with the same answers can be judged differently, as either correct or incorrect, depending on the test questions or the grading criteria. To avoid this, a good test must first ensure that the correct answers are strictly defined for each question. Then, the graders must apply the same standard of correctness to all test takers, regardless of who they are. Essay-type and open-ended questions may be more susceptible to subjective interpretation, so they are not the ideal format for testing.

It cannot be said that a test with "incorrect questions with no answer" is a good test. Such tests can only result in either everyone getting the answer wrong or everyone getting the answer right. In this case, the test score will be uniform for all test takers, and the \keytermE{variance} will be zero. Having a variance of zero means that information cannot be obtained from it, so it is considered a bad test.

In other words, a good test is one with consistent evaluation criteria, in which the judgement of correct or incorrect answers is unambiguous regardless of who is grading or where. It should also have a large variance in the results that can be obtained from the test. If you can only create one question, a good test is one in which half of the test-takers answer correctly while the other half answer incorrectly. This maximizes the variance. If you watch how Akinator asks questions, you can see that it selects questions that divide the "question space that the questioner is likely thinking about" in half. For example, the first question might be "Is it a woman?" You can narrow down at least half of the possibilities by answering Yes or No\footnote{Of course, gender is not limited to two categories, and the answer options for Akinator are "Yes", "No", "I don't know", "Probably yes/partly yes", and "Probably not/not really," but for the sake of simplicity, we are limiting the answers to Yes/No.}. When humans play these types of games, they often ask questions that immediately identify the answer as a bad question. For example, on the second or third question, they might ask "Is the answer you're thinking of 'Haruka Ayase'?" If the answer is No, it would not indicate anything useful because there are many other choices. A good test is one with questions that divide the world in half, and then divide it further and further into multiple categories. If you want to create various categories in one test, arrange the difficulty levels of known questions in advance from 1, 2, 3, etc., and ensure that the degree of correct answers is distributed according to the respondents. This will allow you to obtain a large variance = information.

Like this, tests are models for measuring invisible things, and there is an effort to mathematically describe them and reveal their characteristics. This is called \textit{test theory}, and we will now explain \textit{classical test theory}.

\section{Measurement Model}
Classical test theory is a (now classic) theory that serves as the foundation for test theory. Its model can be expressed very simply. The measurement model of classical test theory is as follows.

\[ X = t + e \]

How simple it is. Here, $X$ represents the test score. Also, $t$ is called the true score, and $e$ is the error. Please think of $t$ as what you really want to measure, such as true ability or true skill. What the classical test theory model is saying is that the test score reflects true ability but has an error added to it. This separation of error and true score is an important point. For example, in hard sciences like physics, measurements target things without a mind, and measurements are made with highly accurate sensors and so on, so measured values are considered to be approximately correct. If a bathroom scale shows 83 kg, then the weight is 83 kg and it is unlikely to be 60 kg or 90 kg. However, things like academic ability or personality are reactions of things assumed to have a mind. Humans can lie, make mistakes, behave as if they are outwardly extroverted, make careless calculation errors, and may respond "that might be true" if someone says "don't you have a sloppy side?" So, in that sense, answers (responses or test scores) that appear in tables cannot be trusted as they are. The meaning of this model is to think that what is visible as $X$ is something that has an added $e$ due to some circumstances, even though it is originally $t$.

It may sound negative to say that one should not trust measurements as they are, but acknowledging the fact that the accuracy of measurements is not very high demonstrates the conscience of the soft sciences. As you are conducting research on human beings, please be aware of this point. (Note: From my personal experience, when I talk to people from other fields, especially those from scientific areas, I am sometimes surprised by their attitude of blindly using the measured values. As a psychologist, I naturally think that "just because the respondents answered, it does not necessarily mean that it is correct". However, some people believe that the answers are correct as they are. It is always better to be aware that those numbers may be wrong and that the psychological factors of the respondent may have influenced the numbers. When setting numerical targets, it is sometimes pointless to measure as people can find out ways to manipulate them, as discussed in detail by \citet{Jerry2019}.)


\section{Fundamental Properties of Errors}
Let's consider the concept of error. Error represents the deviation from what we really want to measure.
For example, suppose there is a person who weighs 80kg, and when they step on a scale, it reads 81kg. This difference of 1kg is the margin of error for this measurement.
By the way, what if the scale of this weighing machine shows 0.5kg even when nobody is on it? In other words, even without any weight, it is off by plus 0.5kg, so a plus 0.5kg deviation will always occur no matter who steps on it. This means that the weighing machine is broken and needs to be adjusted so that it can be reset to zero. This kind of error, which always occurs, is called a systematic error. While it is somewhat inevitable for such errors to be present, if there is a consistent deviation, it can be corrected by subtracting it from the measurement.

What could be the reason for the remaining 0.5kg difference in this person's measurement? It could be an inconsistent, random error, such as having eaten a lot before the measurement, or wearing thick underwear by chance. This type of error is called a "random error". The reason for this error is unknown, as it is a matter of chance. There could be factors that cause it to be positive, or factors that cause it to be negative. This random error is a problematic issue that can occur during measurement. Systematic errors can be adjusted and corrected, but there is no way to deal with random errors due to their lack of consistency.

The error $e$ that must be taken into account during testing is basically a random error, also known as chance error. There will always be factors that prevent the true ability from being fully expressed, such as accidentally finding the topic you had studied the day before or a question on an item you missed from the wordbook that happened to be on the test, or having good or bad health conditions at the time of the test. It is necessary to consider these factors.

Now, let's return to the model of test theory and consider this issue. We can extend the original model slightly and explicitly indicate that it is a test for individual $i$ by adding the subscript $X_i$.
\[ X_i = t_i + e_i \]
Assuming that this test was taken by a class of $n$ people, we can calculate the average score of the class from there.
\[ \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \]

\subsection{Evaluation of Reliability}
How should we evaluate the reliability of a test? In considering this, the nature of errors also provides a clue. Although the average of errors is zero by chance, the variance of the errors is not zero. The variance represents the width of the scatter, which is in turn the width of the fluctuation of the score. For example, assume that a person weighing 80kg steps on the scale and it shows 80.1kg. In this case, there is an error of 100g. Now let's say the person steps off the scale and weighs again, resulting in 79.9kg. This time there is an error of -100g. If it were a household scale, it might be acceptable for a difference of 100g to occur frequently. However, if it were $\pm$1kg, it would be considered a relatively large error. If it were $\pm$3kg or $\pm$5kg, then the scale would be deemed "unreliable". Thus, the reliability refers to the stability of the measurement. This is because errors should not be correlated with anything else and should not have a consistent trend. Therefore, it can be considered that there is no correlation between the error that occurred at the first measurement and the error that occurred at the second measurement. The errors do not correlate with each other in multiple measurements, so after several measurements, the elements of the errors cancel each other out and the true score is obtained.

The reliability of a test can be evaluated by calculating the correlation coefficient of multiple measurements for the same thing, as shown here. If a test is conducted once, and then the same test is conducted again after a short period of time, and the correlation coefficient between these two tests is sufficiently high, then the test can be considered to be reliable. This method of evaluation is called "Test-Retest reliability."

By the way, the important thing to keep in mind is that if the object we are trying to measure is a physical characteristic such as weight, it's fine. However, in the case of psychology, it's the psychological characteristics of humans that we are trying to measure. For example, with academic ability, if we give a test once and do the same test after a three day break, the test-taker may remember the previous questions and prepare accordingly. This means that the object being measured in the first and second tests is not exactly the same, and we may not know which one was the true academic ability. Similarly, in a personality test, the test-taker may remember their answer pattern from the first time and think "I should answer the same as last time," or conversely, "I should answer differently than last time." In this way, the object being measured may change intentionally or unintentionally. It's important that scores are given in the same state when measuring, but be aware that the object of measurement may change constantly. Of course, it's important to consider measures such as allowing memory to fade or creating mechanisms that make it difficult to lie, but it's also a difficult task to do this every time.

Instead of administering the same test twice, it is proposed to create and administer two different tests of equivalent content. For example, one could create 50 math problems to test math proficiency and administer two tests in this format to compare their correlation, thereby establishing their test reliability through what is known as parallel test reliability.

The challenge here is to create two tests with similar difficulty levels. For instance, it is not too difficult to adjust the numbers in a math problems test to achieve the same difficulty level, but with personality tests, one must search for words that measure similar aspects, which may not always result in tests with equivalent difficulty levels. Therefore, the \keytermE{split-half method} can be employed, where a single test is created and conducted, then divided into arbitrary halves such as the first and second halves, or even and odd-numbered questions, and the correlation coefficient is calculated. This enables the assessment of reliability using only one test.

If you can check by splitting it in half, why don't you try splitting it in half again? If you divide it into quarters and take the correlation coefficient for all combinations and average them, it seems to be able to suppress the variation caused by the difference in division. How about if you do that with half? And then half again? ...
When considering this, ultimately it becomes clear that we can calculate the extent to which a particular question (item) in the test correlates with all other items, and take the average of that correlation, in order to measure the reliability based on this approach. The reliability index calculated based on this idea is known as the \keytermE{$\alpha$ coefficient}{アルファけいすう} or Cronbach's alpha. This is represented by the following formula, where M represents the number of items in the test, $V_t$ represents the total variance of the test, and $V_j$ represents the variance of item j.

\[  \alpha =   \frac{M}{M-1} \times \left(1-\frac{\sum V_j}{V_t} \right) \]

This method evaluates the consistency of the entire test by testing the correlation coefficient pairs of each item. In other words, it evaluates the internal consistency reliability of the test. This reliability is also known as internal consistency reliability, and is represented by a value between 0 and 1. A value of around 0.8 is generally considered necessary, but as the formula shows, the value increases when the variance of each item is reduced and the variance of the entire test is increased. Additionally, a larger number of items makes it easier for the value to increase.

In any case, it can be seen that the reliability of the entire test can be evaluated numerically using this method. However, as for whether this actually checks if we are measuring what we want to measure accurately, unfortunately the answer is no.

\subsection{Validity}
It is without question that it is desirable to have measurements that are stable and reliable, meaning that they are consistent over time. However, even if a measurement is reliable, it is meaningless if it lacks \textit{validity}, which refers to the extent to which it accurately captures the target that is intended to be measured.
For example, let's say you want to measure your height and you use a scale. As you grow, your height will increase, but it's also related to your weight, so your weight will increase as your height grows. Although scales are stable measuring devices and quite reliable, can we say that height is being measured with a scale? As they are related variables, we could partially say Yes, but it's still better to measure height with a height gauge. A height gauge will accurately capture the specific characteristic of height and provide a measurement closer to the essence of height.

In the case of height, the definition "height is the height of one's back" is clear and universally understood to the point where nobody questions what height is. The essence of height that everyone believes in - the height of one's back - can be clearly measured with a height measurement tool. However, the concept that psychology is trying to measure is not a concept with a clear definition or a universally understood concept. There are various arguments about what the essence of that concept is. For example, if we define "individuality" as the degree of self-realization, what exactly is self-realization? Is it the degree to which one can do what they want to do on their own? If I can cut my nails when I want to and take a bath when I want to, does that mean I have achieved self-realization? I think most people would find that a little doubtful. Let's consider something more concrete, such as communication skills. Communication skills, literally speaking, are the ability to communicate with others. However, communication involves exchanging information between the speaker and the listener, and it is not enough for the speaker to speak unilaterally or for the listener to just draw out the speaker's words. Even if someone has strong communication skills, they may remain silent during strict formal events such as weddings and funerals. If they continue to remain silent, it may be reasonable to assume that they have no communication skills. If this seems like sophistry, please try to think of a definition of communication skills that everyone can agree on, and you will quickly come up with exceptions and scenes left out of the definition, making it clear that it is not a clear-cut concept.

Much of what psychologists are trying to measure can vary greatly depending on the situation or individual differences, making it difficult to make a stable (reliable) measurement. Furthermore, reliability alone does not compensate for whether the measured value is really the desired value. It is important to define it properly, using a generally accepted concept that is understood by all, and to ensure that the measurement is consistent with the defined state. This is called validity. Although reliability can be quantified using correlation coefficients, validity is essentially difficult to quantify and can only be accepted through argumentation and consideration. Validity is said to be the upper limit of reliability but it is important to make measurements that are both reliable and valid.

\subsection{Evaluating Validity}
Validity is evaluated from several perspectives. Here, I will explain three representative evaluation criteria.
\paragraph{Construct Validity} Validity is the degree to which a concept accurately captures its essence, so we must first consider what that essence is. Since psychologists aim to measure the nature of the mind, we must define this nature precisely and then evaluate whether measurements align with that definition; this is known as \keytermE{construct validity}. However, before even assessing whether a concept is a valid construct, we must first question whether it is appropriate to focus on that particular nature of the mind. For example, there may be doubt as to whether it makes sense to assume that there is such a thing as communication ability (as was previously given as a poor example), or to emphasize gender differences by using such concepts as "girl power," or to accept weakly-evidenced theories such as the "gaming brain." In order to increase construct validity, prior research must be examined to determine how a particular concept differs from others and why it is necessary to present this concept. Without these explanations, confusing jargon may proliferate and require caution.

\paragraph{Criterion-related validity} When considering psychological constructs, if they are truly useful concepts, they should be reflected in some practical responses or behaviors. At that time, other indicators related to the concept should also change together. For example, some people may deny that the state of depression is "being spoiled", but there are facts that clients with depressive symptoms cannot eat or cry for no reason. Taking them into consideration as a whole, a doctor makes a diagnosis. Now, let's say we create a questionnaire in psychology to measure the degree of depression. By answering several items and scoring them, if the score is high, the state of depression can be judged to be high. If we ask clients who have already been medically diagnosed with depression and those who have not to answer this questionnaire, there should be a difference in the scores. Criterion-related validity is judged by comparing what is measured against external criteria to see if they correspond or not.

\paragraph{Content validity} Refers to whether the created measurement items are able to comprehensively capture the content that we intend to measure. For instance, let's say we want to measure personality. Since personality comprises of various aspects, we need to approach it from multiple angles with various items. We need to check whether we have accurately captured everything that we intend to measure such as whether someone is serious, has a short temper or enjoys speaking with others. One method to check content validity is to analyse whether results from statistical analysis agree with the hypothetical model. For example, by using the factor analysis method, we can determine whether the number of dimensions matches the obtained dimensions of the items and whether their correspondence is appropriate. Besides content validity, factor analysis can also be used to test other types of validity such as factor validity, discriminant validity and convergent validity. Note that more detailed explanation will be provided in the applied class of the second year.

Validity has various aspects, and it is sometimes argued whether there is validity in the concept of many validity concepts. For more information on validity, please refer to Chapter 4 of Kosugi (2016) and take a look at it.

However, the scary thing about psychology is that the person you are asking the questions to is human. Humans make mistakes, lie, and take the easy way out. Even without malice, they may respond with "let me just answer randomly" or "I'll just circle any number between one and five" if they don't understand what they are being asked. It is rare to get a blank answer sheet, but you cannot be sure if they truly understand and are answering correctly. Even a simple typo on a survey can turn off a respondent and discourage them from providing accurate answers. Additionally, a researcher may become so fixated on their hypothesis that they unconsciously find results that support it, a phenomenon known as "Teppei's Magic" in the field. For more information, see \citet{kosugi2018}.

Psychology is a serious academic discipline, unlike "psychological tests" that are used for entertainment purposes. Only concepts that have been carefully considered and analyzed are accumulated as knowledge for future generations. Furthermore, since the subject of study is something intangible and profound, research methods need to be more refined and analysis must be more objective and accurate.
