Even if you think Bayesian estimation seems good, it's no use if you can't actually calculate it by hand. So in the end, I'll explain a practical example of Bayesian estimation.
If it is a simple model, it is possible to obtain the answer analytically from the calculation of the probability distribution, but here I will explain the method using MCMC algorithm for considering future situations. This method is provided in the \texttt{brms} package \citep{brmsCite1, brmsCite2} of R.
Using the \texttt{brms} package in R makes it easy to implement generalized linear models. Simply changing the function name from \texttt{lm} to \texttt{brm} allows for Bayesian estimation of linear models using MCMC, with easy visualization of the results. Like the \texttt{lm} function, having the explanatory variables as discrete (factor) variables allows for estimation of the size of effects of factors, and the \texttt{conditional_effects} function in the \texttt{brms} package can be used to visualize marginalized results by each factor.

"Evaluation of MCMC method"
As stated earlier, MCMC is a random number generation method that draws a histogram of results by sampling from the posterior distribution. This is because the histogram draws the ridge line of the probability distribution. However, since it uses random numbers, the result may be different every time. It is also necessary to check if the random numbers are truly from the posterior distribution. Information about this is written around the 6th line of the output (\texttt{console\ref{RS:out27_03}}).
\input{text1_chap27old_escape_11}
"Let's decipher this and read it."

The MCMC method proceeds step-by-step, producing the next random number one step at a time. The MCMC samples are the representative candidates for the parameters that need to be estimated. Figure \ref{fig::27_02} shows the extracted data saved internally. The values of the random numbers obtained at each step are written for the intercept (\verb|b_intercept|), slope (\texttt{b}), and scatter of error (\texttt{sigma}). For example, the slope goes from $77.55104 \to 100.42599 \to 133.46551 \to 77.90979 \to \cdots$, producing the next random number based on the previous value. There are actually three variables here, and they are sampled from the joint probability space consisting of these three variables, enclosed in a red frame. In other words, three sets are sampled in each step, such as $(336.0793,77.55104,32.49790)\to(283.5139,100.42599,41.52797)\to\cdots$. This is accumulated step by step, and the jagged line in the middle of the figure shows the steps. The histogram, which is obtained by summarizing and accumulating it, becomes the posterior distribution, which is the basic principle.

\input{text1_chap27old_escape_12}


Based on this, let's take a look at the output. First, it says "4 chains". This means that MCMC executed 4 steps in parallel. MCMC proceeds step by step, generating the next random number one step at a time. However, depending on where it starts, there is a possibility that it will end up with a completely different answer. Even in the middle of a step, it may have wandered off to a strange place. To check these concerns, instead of running a single Markov chain, multiple chains are run at the same time, and it is determined that "each chain reaches the same place" to ensure that sampling is done correctly.

Next is \texttt{iter}. This is the total number of steps, and in this case, it means that each chain has taken 2000 steps. Next, \texttt{warmup} is the warm-up period or burn-in period. The steps at the beginning of the chain may depend on the initial value and may not be suitable as representative values of the posterior distribution. To represent the posterior distribution properly, the first period is not used, and it is recorded that this is the first 1000 steps. For each chain, 2000 steps are taken, and 1000 steps are used because the first 1000 steps are discarded. However, since there are four chains, only 1000 steps are accepted. This means that there are a total of 4000 samples. The posterior distribution is drawn with a histogram of 4000 points. \texttt{total post-warmup samples = 4000} means that. Finally, there is \texttt{thin=1}, which means thinning. This means the interval between steps used as samples. In this case, it is 1, so every sample (1 step at a time) is collected, but if it is 2 or 3, it means that the results are only saved every 2 or 3 steps.

Please refer to Figure \ref{fig::27_03}. This figure is called a trace plot and is the one that was in the middle of the previous Figure \ref{fig::27_02}, or the one on the right hand side of the output from \texttt{plot} in Figure \ref{fig::27_01}. The plot visualizes where the chain wanders while connecting the steps of the chain along the horizontal axis. The figure above shows that even when starting from multiple starting points, the chain wraps around and samples the same area. On the other hand, the chain is disentangled in the lower part of the figure. Such a case is evidence that sampling did not go well, indicating that there was some problem such as a bad model, insufficient conditions for identifying the parameters, and so on.

\section{Estimation of Within-Group Model}
Finally, I will explain the group model. Please recall the intervention effects model from Chapter \ref{chapter22}. The linear model with group factors can be represented as follows.
\[ X_{ij} = \mu_i + \beta_0 + \beta_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ijk}\]
The important point here is that, unlike the model of inter-group factors, individual mean values $\mu_i$ are assumed. Writing this as a probabilistic model, we can express it as follows:
\[ X_{ij} \sim Normal( \mu_i + \beta_0 + \beta_{ij} ,\sigma)\]
This is where it gets average, represented by a linear model, please confirm that the scatter of error $\varepsilon$ is expressed by the standard deviation $\sigma$ of the normal distribution. There are no other differences.
By the way, the individual difference $i$ represents the starting state before intervention, and it is considered to take various values. However, we can also assume that the distribution of this individual part follows a normal distribution. In other words,
\[ \mu_i \sim Normal(\mu_G, \tau) \]
As for the standard deviation representing individual variation, a new symbol $\tau$ (tau) has been prepared. To express this in a linear model, it is equivalent to considering the hierarchical structure where a normal distribution is enclosed within another normal distribution.
