Analysis using JASP was relatively easy and easy to understand, wasn't it? However, that may be thanks to the user interface. That is, thanks to the GUI that allows for visual manipulation through clicks, taps, and drops, making it easy and convenient to use.
Of course, I'm not denying it, but one disadvantage of GUI is the difficulty in sharing what you've done with others. It's difficult to explain movements in words like "Do this like this...". Inability to share leads to irreproducibility, and in severe cases, it can also lead to situations like "The machine did it on its own, so I don't know what I did".\footnote{In fact, what's troublesome when teaching statistical software with GUI is that people may unconsciously do strange analysis by violating the principles or assumptions of statistics because they are actually doing partitioning under the default conditions or they can not remember the movements they made themselves.} On the other hand, methods such as leaving execution instructions with CUI, such as R, may seem cumbersome at first glance, but they are suitable for research and educational purposes due to the recording and high reproducibility.

Well, if you're wondering if it's possible to do Bayesian estimation in R, the answer is of course yes. Let's go through the process of how to do it through some exercises. Although, all you need to do is use a package that supports Bayesian analysis.
If the model is simple, it is possible to obtain the answer analytically by calculating the probability distribution. However, in consideration of the future, I will explain the method using MCMC. This is provided in the "brms" package \citep{brmsCite1,brmsCite2} of R.
Using the R package \texttt{brms}, it is easy to implement a generalized linear model. Simply changing the function name from \texttt{lm} to \texttt{brm} allows for Bayesian estimation of a linear model using MCMC sampling, and facilitates easy visualization of the results. Just like with the \texttt{lm} function, by treating the explanatory variables as discrete (factor) variables, the effects of the factors' magnitudes can be estimated. The \texttt{brms} package also includes the \verb|conditional_effects| function to display the marginalized results by factor.

\subsection{Evaluation of MCMC methods}
As mentioned earlier, MCMC is a method of generating random numbers to draw a histogram of results by sampling from the posterior distribution. This is because the histogram can draw the contours of the probability distribution. However, because it is a random number, different results are obtained every time. It is necessary to check whether the random number is truly from the posterior distribution. Information on this is written around the 6th line of the result (\texttt{Output from R\ref{RS:out27_03}}). Let's decipher this information.

\input{m29_brms_escape_11}

The MCMC method generates the next random number step by step. The MCMC samples are potential representative candidates for the parameters to be estimated. Figure \ref{fig::27_02} shows the data extracted from the saved internal data, which represents the values of the generated random numbers at each step for the intercept (\verb|b_intercept|), slope (\texttt{b}), and the degree of scattering of the error (\texttt{sigma}) that we want to estimate. For example, the slope is generated sequentially based on the previous value, such as from $77.55104$ to $100.42599$ to $133.46551$ to $77.90979$ and so on. In reality, there are three variables, and we sample from the joint probability space consisting of these three variables as indicated by the red square. In other words, we take steps of three, as in $(336.0793,77.55104,32.49790) \to (283.5139,100.42599,41.52797) \to \cdots$. The jagged line in the middle of the figure shows the steps that accumulate over time, and the histogram that is stacked up at the end is obtained as the posterior distribution. This is the basic principle.

\input{m29_brms_escape_12}


Based on this, let's take a look at the output. First, it says \texttt{4 chains}. This means that we ran 4 parallel steps of MCMC. MCMC proceeds step by step, generating the next random number one by one, but depending on where it starts, it may end up with completely different answers. Even in the middle of a step, it may have wandered off to a strange place. To check these concerns, we run multiple chains simultaneously rather than just one Markov chain, and judge that correct sampling has been performed by saying, "Every chain reaches the same place."

The next parameter is \texttt{iter}. This refers to the total number of steps, and in this case it means that each chain made 2000 steps. The parameter \texttt{warmup} refers to the warm-up period, also known as the burn-in period. The first few steps of a chain can depend on the initial values and may not be representative of the posterior distribution. To ensure that the posterior distribution is well-represented, the initial period is usually discarded, with the first 1000 steps being recorded for this purpose. For each chain, 2000 steps are taken, but the first 1000 are discarded, resulting in only 1000 steps being retained. As there are four chains, a total of 4000 samples are obtained. The posterior distribution is then plotted using a histogram with 4000 points. The parameter \texttt{total post-warmup samples = 4000} indicates this. Finally, the parameter \texttt{thin=1} refers to thinning, which means the interval at which steps are saved as samples. In this case, it is set to 1, so every step is saved as a sample. However, if it were set to 2 or 3, then only every 2nd or 3rd step would be saved as a sample.

Please refer to Figure \ref{fig::27_03}. This is what is called a trace plot, which appeared in the middle of the previous Figure \ref{fig::27_02}, or on the right side of the Figure \ref{fig::27_01} when plotted using \texttt{plot}. It visualizes where the chain is wandering around by connecting the steps of the chain with lines, while the number of steps is taken as the horizontal axis. The top of the figure shows that even if the chain starts from multiple starting points, it is sampling similar regions by getting tangled up with each other. On the other hand, the chains in the lower part of the plot are untangled. Such cases indicate that the sampling did not work well, revealing that there was some problem, such as a bad model or insufficient conditions to identify the parameters.
\input{m29_brms_escape_13}
In this way, it is necessary to check the behavior of MCMC after the results are output. The output screen states that if the $\hat{R}$ (Rhat) for evaluating the entanglement of chains is 1, then it has converged. The Rhat column in the results for this time is all 1.00, so it can be diagnosed that MCMC is successfully obtaining representative values from the posterior distribution (\texttt{R output \ref{RS:out27_04}}) without modifying the TeX code.

\section{Estimating Group-level Models}
Finally, let me explain the within-group models. Please recall the intervention effect model from Lesson \ref{m22_anovaOnR}. As you may remember, a linear model with within-group factor can be represented as follows:
\[ X_{ij} = \mu_i + \beta_0 + \beta_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ijk}\]
The key point here is that, unlike in the model with between-group factors, there is an assumption that there is a specific mean value $\mu_i$ for each individual $i$. To express this as a probability model:
\[ X_{ij} \sim Normal( \mu_i + \beta_0 + \beta_{ij} ,\sigma)\]
Please translate this Japanese text into English without modifying the TeX code:

This is represented by a linear model where the average is hit, and the error ε is scattered, and confirmed to be represented by the standard deviation σ of the normal distribution. There is no other difference.
By the way, the individual difference $i$ represents the starting status before intervention, and can take various values, but it is also possible to assume a normal distribution for this personality aspect. In other words,
\[ \mu_i \sim Normal(\mu_G, \tau) \]
