\section{Prior Distribution and Posterior Distribution}
Let's consider an example of actually estimating parameters using the Bayesian method.

As an example, we'll focus on academic testing.
Let's say you have taken a test of 10 questions with the same level of difficulty. What you want to estimate is the test taker's ability. This ability is defined as the proportion of correct answers to the questions, denoted as $\theta$. Since this ability cannot be observed directly, all we can observe is the number of correct answers. So, if a participant answers 4 questions correctly, how can we estimate their ability? This is the task at hand.

The Bayesian approach to reasoning basically consists of only two steps\footnote{That's fewer than hypothesis testing! Way to go!}.

Let's first consider Step 1, the prior distribution.
\subsection{Expressing Prior Beliefs}
The prior distribution is a distribution given in advance of the probability parameter. One might think it strange to have prior knowledge about something that we want to find out, namely how the parameter values will be.
Here, please consider the Bayesian approach to probability as expressing the degree of certainty. We express the degree of uncertainty or lack of knowledge using probability. Inference is the redistribution of probability, and if there are no other possibilities, we can say that it is 100% certain. Conversely, if we have no idea where the parameter is, we can also express it using probability. As a way of expression, if we don't know "where" it is, we can widen the range of defense by saying that "everything is possible". Since it is a probability, the area must be 1.0, but we can simply think of stretching it thinly accordingly.

\input{m26_bayesian2_escape_02}

In Figure \ref{fig::25_01}, four probability distributions are shown. Please consider these as representing individuals' beliefs about the parameter $\theta$ of the Bernoulli distribution. The Bernoulli distribution is a probability distribution for a trial that produces binary outcomes, like flipping a coin, so the probability $\theta$ should be in the range $0 \le \theta \le 1$.
The upper left corner of Figure \ref{fig::25_01} is flat across the entire range. In other words, it represents a state of uncertainty where it cannot be claimed that it is located "where" and should be equally present everywhere, in the belief that nothing clear can be said, i.e., an unknown state. When analyzing data, if nothing is known beforehand, it is sufficient to place a flat prior distribution (especially a \keytermE{uniform distribution}) like this to represent an uninformative prior distribution.
The upper right of Figure \ref{fig::25_01} is thought to be around $\theta = 0.5$, but in reverse. At least it should be around 0.45 and at most around 0.55, which means that we can say with high certainty (narrow probability distribution) that it is in a fairly confident state.
I'm not sure exactly, but someone with a slightly vague and low expectation of the outcome might express their belief as shown in the bottom left of Figure 25.01, and someone who strongly believes that the outcome will only be a surface and not a table will be represented as shown in the bottom right of Figure 25.01, without modifying the TeX code.

In this way, it is possible to represent prior information as a probability distribution, which may include things that are completely unknown, have a strong attachment to a certain idea, or just a vague idea of where to begin. In practical research settings, if there is "absolutely nothing" that is known beforehand, a uniform distribution such as a non-informative prior distribution or a weakly informative prior distribution with a wide range can be used. When analyzing data such as height and weight, we assume a normal distribution, but in this case, there should not be negative values and even if someone is very tall or heavy, they should not exceed 10m or 5t, so it is okay to have a prior distribution with a range of $0\le \mu \le 100000$. Alternatively, if there is a certain degree of knowledge based on previous research, a loosely scattered prior distribution can be placed around that value. In other words, by placing a prior distribution like this, it is possible to maintain consistency with previous research and utilize information from it to accumulate knowledge. It should be noted, however, that R.A. Fisher, who came up with analysis of variance and maximum likelihood methods, did not favor this Bayesian approach. He believed that having any preconceived ideas before starting research was ridiculous and that one should approach data with no mindset. His strong dislike for Bayesian statistics is also noted in \citet{mcgrayne2011theory}.

Let's go back to the present example. We have no idea how much knowledge this test taker has beforehand. However, considering the accuracy rate, the estimated value should be within the range of $0 \leq \theta \leq 1.$ Since we have no idea, let's set a uniform distribution within this range as the non-informative prior distribution. This means that we express the spirit of confronting data without any bias with a non-informative prior distribution.


\subsection{Calculation of posterior distribution}
Next is step 2. It's an update. We'll use Bayes' theorem for the update. Let's review Bayes' theorem again. It's the following equation, right?
\[ P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}\]
I only mentioned that $P()$ represents probability, but whether it is discrete or continuous, it is called a probability distribution. In particular, since the left side means "ultimately becomes like this", it is called the posterior distribution.

Let's consider using this probabilistic relationship (both the right and left sides are enclosed with P!) in actual statistical inference. The posterior distribution on the left expresses, in conditional probability, inferring where the parameter is located based on collected data. In other words, when B represents data and A represents parameters, the posterior distribution $P(A|B)$ denotes "the probability of the parameter given the collected data." Then, the $P(B|A)$ on the right means "the probability of obtaining the data when the parameter is present." What was that again? Ah, the likelihood!

Now, let's think about the meaning of the symbols. $P(A)$ represents the probability of the parameters before taking the data, which is the \keytermE{prior distribution} that we just explained.

Also, $P(B)$ represents the probability of the data. Probability of the data? The probability of whether the data is obtained? It may seem unclear, but if we recall the example of the breast cancer incidence rate from the previous section, we can imagine it as "the collection of probabilities of all possible data that can be obtained from the possible parameters." Although it is referred to as the \keytermE{marginal likelihood}, one does not need to delve deeply into this concept in practical use, which will be explained later. So, we will stop talking about this for now\footnote{To be precise, the marginal likelihood is an important factor when considering the fitness of the data. However, like the derivation of the $t$-distribution, it is not necessary to delve into this concept for a beginner in psychological statistics. If you are interested, please read \citet{Hamada201912}.}.

Now, let's try rewriting Bayes' theorem using words.
\[ \text{Posterior distribution} = \text{Likelihood} \times \frac{\text{Prior distribution}}{\text{Marginal likelihood}}\]
Please note that as shown here, the left-hand side becomes a distribution when multiplied by the likelihood. As we discussed last time, likelihood is not probability. Its function does not integrate to 1.0, therefore it is not a probability. However, this formula multiplies the likelihood by the prior distribution, divides by the marginal likelihood, and obtains the posterior distribution. The posterior distribution is a probability distribution. In other words, one could say that Bayes' theorem transforms likelihood into probability.

Now, Bayes' theorem states that the posterior distribution is equal to the likelihood multiplied by the prior distribution, divided by the marginal likelihood. In this case, the likelihood is that the individual answered 4 questions correctly, without knowing the specific order of their answers. So, it could be either correct, correct, correct, correct, incorrect, incorrect, incorrect, incorrect, incorrect, incorrect, or some other random order. Since this is a Binomial distribution, the likelihood can be calculated as $\theta^4(1-\theta)^6$.

It may also be helpful to think of it as extracting finer numbers along the x-axis and multiplying them one by one (see figure \ref{fig::25_03}). By the way, the prior distribution used in this case is a non-informative uniform distribution, so although its height changes when combined with the likelihood, the overall shape does not. This means that using a uniform distribution for the prior allows for reflecting the relationship between data and probability distribution, as well as the shape of the likelihood, directly in the results.
\input{m26_bayesian2_escape_04}
Now, let's call the multiplied result $P(D)$, which is the probability of the data. However, let's ignore this denominator for now. This is because, if we think about what this denominator is doing, we can see that it does not contain any changing numbers (variables) like parameters, which means it is a "constant". The numerator is the product of the likelihood and the prior distribution, so the numbers should be very small. However, in the end, the left-hand side is the probability distribution called the posterior distribution. This means that the left-hand side is normalized to an area of 1.0, and the denominator is just a constant used for division to make the area 1.0.

We want to know where the parameters are located, but "this seems to be here" means that the probability there is high. Therefore, if we know the shape, we can easily estimate the position on the horizontal axis even if we don't know the units on the vertical axis well. Thus, let's ignore the denominator. (Note: Actually, as we dive deeper into Bayesian inference, this denominator is quite interesting, and it can be used as a measure of how well the data fits the model. But that's another story.) \section{Representative numbers of posterior distributions}
This is the estimation method using Bayesian inference, which calculates the result in this way. The resulting posterior distribution takes the form shown in the lower right of Figure \ref{fig::25_04}.
You may not immediately understand what is meant by "this is the result". Bayesian methods provide not only an estimate, but also the posterior distribution as the probability distribution. Although the method of moments or maximum likelihood estimation can provide an answer such as "the value of $\theta$ is XXX", in the case of Bayesian estimation, the answer is given as a "distribution", so it's not possible to give a single value for the answer. The entire distribution is the answer, and it can be said that it represents the strength of the belief that "it is likely to be around here" just as the interpretation of the prior distribution.

You can certainly look at the distribution as a whole and say "Hmm, interesting." However, you can also use the representative values of the distribution in reporting your results.
For example, let's consider the peak of this distribution function, which is equivalent to the mode in descriptive statistics. Since this is the most probable point, it can be considered as the most plausible or maximum likelihood estimate. By the way, the maximum likelihood estimate of this function is called the MAP estimate, which stands for Maximum A Posterior, and the estimate based on the expected value of the probability function, which is called the EAP estimate, is represented by the Expectation A Posterior acronym. Since this function is skewed to the left, the median value, represented by the MEDian a posterior estimation or MED estimate, might be more appropriate than the EAP estimate for representing the central tendency. By the way, the peak of this function in this case, the MAP estimate, is $\theta=0.4$, which is the most probable point.

I have drawn numbers summarizing the distribution in Figure \ref{fig::26_05}.
\input{m26_bayesian2_escape_06}

As a summary of the distribution, we can also use indicators that express the range. As statistical measures for expressing the range, there are "quantiles" and "percentiles". Quantiles divide the data into four segments at the 25\%, 50\%, and 75\% points and report the values corresponding to 25\% and 75\%. The second quartile, which corresponds to the 50\% point, is the same as the median. Percentiles divide the data at any arbitrary percentage point and report the values accordingly.  Reporting the values at the 2.5\% and 97.5\% points covers the entire 95\% interval, so this 95\% range is often reported. These ranges of posterior distribution are specifically called "credible intervals."

By the way, the shape of a distribution, such as in the case of posterior distributions, is not always symmetric like a normal distribution. The confidence interval that takes 2.5\% from each tail of the distribution is called an \textit{equal-tailed interval} (ETI). In contrast, the \textit{highest-density interval} (HDI) estimates the most credible region of a distribution and covers most of the distribution, with every point inside the interval having a higher density than any point outside of the interval. Although HDI is more precise, ETI is more commonly used because it is easier to compute.

I created a figure explaining the differences between ETI and HDI in Figure \ref{fig::26_05}. I created a distribution that is slightly skewed to the left, and the 75\% ETI range is $[0.0886, 0.266]$ while the HDI range is $[0.075, 0.242]$. ETI is the width that equally divides the remaining area on the left and right sides, so the area on the left side is slightly larger. On the other hand, HDI is a range where the density in that interval is higher than in other intervals, so a relatively equal width is obtained. Note that there is a subtle difference numerically, but there is a difference in thinking\footnote{To draw this interval, I used the \verb|eti| and \verb|hdi| functions in the \verb|bayestestR| package in R.}.

Using these numbers, we will summarize the posterior distribution. Since the MAP estimate this time was 0.4, the probability of the posterior probability being 0.4 is the highest. In other words, we can assume that the estimate for this person is 0.4.

You might think, "What is that?" Out of 10 questions, getting 4 correct yields an average of 0.4, which is the answer using the method of moments using the sample average. Since the shape of the likelihood function and the posterior distribution are the same, the maximum likelihood estimation result will also be 0.4. However, in the case of maximum likelihood estimation, it cannot be said that the probability of being 0.4 is the highest "probability." This is because the likelihood function is not a probability, so it can only be said that being 0.4 is the most reasonable. In Bayesian estimation, it is okay to say that the probability of the posterior distribution being 0.4 is the highest. What is important is that no matter which method is used, the answers will be similar. It is because they are estimating the same parameter with the same data, even though the methods and expressions may differ. Please keep in mind the differences in the process and expression of each estimation method, and what can be said.

In the case of Bayesian inference, the advantage is that the answer is expressed in terms of probability, so it is easy to express. For example, while the MAP estimate is 0.4, it is possible to express statements such as, "the probability that the value of $\theta$ is between 0.306 and 0.499 is 50%" or "there is a 95% probability that $\theta$ is distributed between 0.159 and 0.682," as shown in Figure \ref{fig::25_05}. Please note that this interval estimation method is different from the confidence interval using the moment method. Estimation by the moment method uses standard errors to estimate and "asserts that it is within this interval," meaning that the probability of hitting this declaration is 95%. In reality, it is a binary choice of whether it is accurate or a miss, and essentially a point estimate, while this method is essentially an interval estimate, meaning that it can only be said that "it is within this interval," or that "the confidence level of being in this interval is 95%." This interval by Bayesian inference is called a "credible interval," distinguishing it from the "confidence interval" of frequentist statistics\footnote{However, they are both abbreviated as C.I. in English, and although there are subtle differences, it is convenient to say CI95 because it can be interpreted either way... or maybe not? \section{Three Estimation Methods}
The three estimation methods, Moment Method, Maximum Likelihood Method, and Bayesian Method have now all been introduced without modification to the TeX code. Let's review their respective characteristics once again.

The first method discussed was the method of moments, which is a method that focuses on descriptive statistics such as using the sample mean as an estimate of the population mean and the unbiased sample variance as an estimate of the population variance. This method assumes a normal distribution for the population and expresses the estimation accuracy using the distribution of the sample statistics (see Figure \ref{fig::26_01}). This approach is based on the idea that if you repeat the sampling indefinitely, the sample will eventually converge to a single value, which is an extension of the method of defining probabilities as the limit of an infinite sequence of trials.
\input{m26_bayesian2_escape_08}
This is a method that has been built up over time and is widely used in statistical packages because the procedure is standardized. The null hypothesis, which is well-suited to this method, has been used in psychology and other fields for a long time. However, the standardization of the procedure has led to a misuse of the method, producing misreadings, misunderstandings, and rule violations. The manual itself is also being updated, and there is now an argument that unless there is a good reason, the method should not be used. This is not to say that the null hypothesis test is faulty, but that it is not suitable for exploratory research areas without exact procedures for testing. I hope that everyone handles it with great care.

Now, there is also a method known as Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE), which involves using a probability distribution function to search for parameters that fit the data (Figure 26.02). This was developed by R.A. Fisher, the father of statistical analysis, and involves using the assumption that the data is generated from a population with a normal distribution to search for the most likely parameters given the data. In essence, MLE involves considering which parameters are most likely to produce the observed data.
\input{m26_bayesian2_escape_09}
From the perspective of population and sample, this is a very intuitive idea. It is as if we think of a probability distribution as a data generator, and ask ourselves what the settings or parameters of this machine are that produce this data. The idea is to assume that the parameter value that yields the best possible reproduction of the data we have on hand is the true setting.

The maximum likelihood estimation method is also widely used. It is a standard method for estimation that can handle a large amount of data, as it does not take up too much computational time. This method can be said to be the foundation of probability models. However, it is important to be cautious about the use of the concept of likelihood. Also, as models become more complex, there are computational limits that even modern computers cannot overcome, as there is a possibility that the probability distribution that matches the data may be wrong to begin with.

The third approach is the Bayesian method (Figure \ref{fig::26_03}). The principle of this Bayesian method was developed in the 18th century, but it became practical with the development of computer science, and its use has rapidly spread.
\input{m26_bayesian2_escape_10}
Using this method, even complex models that didn't produce satisfactory answers with maximum likelihood method can give answers without any problem. With maximum likelihood method, there are cases where a stable answer cannot be obtained without a lot of data, but with Bayesian method, answers can be obtained even with little data. Some people say that the only limit for Bayesian analysis is one's imagination, as for those who practice Bayesian statistics are called "Bayesians". By the way, there are various appellations for people who use programming language Python such as "Pythonists", while those who use typesetting software LaTeX are called "TeXnicians". For some reason, people who use R are known as "R Ojisans" (meaning "R grandpas" in Japanese) \citep{Lee201709}.

Why is it possible to obtain answers even with limited data, or with complex models? Technically, the answer is the development of the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method, which is an approximation method based on random number generation. However, there is another reason, which is the presence of a prior distribution that provides "hints" for finding the answer, something that the maximum likelihood method does not have.

\section{Trade-Off Between Prior and Likelihood}
Please remember the (only two) steps of Bayesian estimation.
\input{m26_bayesian2_escape_11}
First, we start with a prior distribution and update it to a posterior distribution. That's all. If we can update the posterior distribution with data, we can use it as the prior distribution for the next analysis. This is a significant feature of Bayesian estimation, where data equals information, and we can use it to advance the analysis with each iteration.

The posterior distribution is formed by the product of the prior distribution and the likelihood. We showed three examples of analysis in Figure \ref{fig::26_04}. Please read the figure vertically; the top row shows the likelihood, and multiplying it by the prior distribution in the second row leads to the shape of the posterior distribution in the third row. In all cases, the relationship between data and probability distribution (i.e., likelihood) is the same, and it takes the form of the Bernoulli likelihood function we saw in the previous example of four successes out of ten trials.
\input{m26_bayesian2_escape_12}
The second row represents the prior distributions, with the leftmost one indicating the belief before analysis that "this person seems to have high ability (high accuracy rate)." In this case, the posterior distribution has a peak between the likelihood peak (0.4) and the prior distribution peak (around 0.9) at the bottom left. On the opposite end, the rightmost prior distribution represents the belief before analysis that "this person definitely has low ability." In this case, the posterior distribution is pulled in the direction of the prior distribution and has a considerably lower peak. The middle column represents the belief that "maybe the person is not that good," and the posterior distribution takes a shape between the likelihood and prior distribution peak. The TeX code remains unchanged.

When interpreting the results, we may wonder whether it is problematic that the results can be so different based on just one choice of prior distribution. This is because the prior distribution could potentially become a "subjective bias" of the analyst. Even if we obtain an answer by placing an arbitrary prior distribution, we may question the objective and fair nature of science if a completely different answer is obtained by placing a different prior distribution. Therefore, it is recommended to determine the prior distribution objectively or to use an uninformative prior distribution.

On the other hand, having a prior distribution means being able to utilize the accumulation of past data. Furthermore, since the posterior distribution is determined by multiplying the prior distribution and the likelihood, if the likelihood is extremely sharp - meaning that a fact is supported by a lot of data - then a gentle prior distribution will not change the shape of the posterior distribution. Conversely, if the prior distribution is extremely sharp - meaning that there is a very strong prior belief or a lot of accumulated knowledge - the result can change significantly with just a little evidence. In that sense, it could be said that the nature of Bayes is fair-minded.
