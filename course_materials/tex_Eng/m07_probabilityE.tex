\chapter{Errors and Probability}
\label{m07_probability}
This time, we'll be talking about probability.

Last time, we talked about psychological measurements. Some people may have wondered why we suddenly started talking about math, but probability and statistics are intimately related.
In psychology, we talked about errors that occur in measurement. These errors are irregular and cannot be predicted as they are invisible to the naked eye.
When it comes to controlling the lack of regularity, the concept of probability comes into play.

The numbers called "probability" can describe the underlying global nature while representing randomness and uncertainty each time.
Even if we don't know the individual facts every time, we can describe the overall trend, as errors in measurement accumulate and the average approaches zero.

This can also be applied when conducting large-scale surveys. That is, even if individual differences are not known, it is possible to grasp the overall average tendency by aggregating and taking statistics.

To handle chance events and control them as an overall trend, the concept of probability cannot be avoided.

\section{Numbers Known as Probability}
You may have learned some probability and statistics in high school mathematics. Starting from counting "possible cases," you may have encountered permutations, combinations... ah, it can be such a hassle! Some people may have this impression.

At the same time, however, the word "probability" is used more commonly in everyday language. For example, you might hear phrases like "there's a 30% chance of precipitation in the forecast" or "we're guaranteed to win 100%!".

Both of these are \keytermE{probabilities}. The conditions that the numbers represented by the term "probability" should follow can actually be summarized in the following three.
\input{m07_probability_escape_01}

Firstly, since probability is a positive number (for example, 10\% is a positive real number expressed as 0.1), the first condition is straightforward.

The second one contains a series of complex terms. An event that actually happens is referred to as an "outcome," and the subset of outcomes considered in a particular situation is called a sample, while the entire set of possible outcomes is called the sample space. So, please understand it as "all possible things" for now. In other words, the second condition refers to the fact that "when you add up all possible probabilities, the result should be 1.0." That's why probability in high school math was about counting all possible outcomes and expressing them as relative frequencies. But, for example, if a weather forecast is limited to three states (events) of "sunny," "rainy," and "cloudy," and the probability of it raining tomorrow is about 0.3, then that's good enough\footnote{There are incorrect ways to use probability expressions in everyday language. For example, former Osaka governor Toru Hashimoto stated in an interview before the 2007 election, "The possibility of my candidacy is 20,000 percent impossible" (and then he changed his mind a few days later and ran for the election, ultimately winning, but let's leave that aside), which is not a probability number. Similarly, the main character of Dr. Stone, Senku, has a catchphrase like "It's 10 billion percent not possible," which is also not a probability number. It's unfortunate dialogue for a well-educated protagonist.}. You might also want to express the strength of your determination or will in terms of probability, but in that case, simply express how strongly you feel out of all possible scenarios you can think of. By the way, a weather forecast is a prediction of the future, and it does not calculate the proportion of weather based on listing all possible futures. Rather, it expresses the strength of the desire for a particular outcome based on past occurrences of similar weather patterns.

Now, the third condition seems a bit complicated. But let's read the words carefully. "Any two mutually exclusive events" means "two events of any kind, but mutually exclusive". Exclusive means that when one occurs, the other does not, so they exclude each other and are not compatible. For example, in the case of probability, the events of rolling a 1 and rolling a 2 on a die are mutually exclusive. It is impossible for both the 1 and 2 faces to be facing up at the same time. Based on this, the latter part refers to the probability of "either one happening". In the previous example, it refers to the state of "rolling a 1 or 2" on a die. In this case, it simply means explaining the sum of the individual probabilities, which is $1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3$.

If these three conditions are met, the number can be called a probability.
The probability learned in high school math is defined, for example, as "the probability of rolling a die is that the relative frequency of the die eventually converges to 1/6 if it is rolled infinitely." Although it is impossible to actually perform an infinite number of trials, if the probability of each outcome is equal, it should eventually reach 1/6, and if the ratio of outcomes is $\{1/6,1/6,1/6,1/6,1/6,1/6 \}$, then since it is non-negative, adds to 1, and satisfies the third condition, it can be called probability. In the case of "probability of precipitation" used as an example as "the probability of rain is 60%, the probability of cloudy is 30%, and the probability of clear weather is 10%," the idea of "if today is repeated infinitely ..." does not hold, but since it satisfies the definition of probability by thinking "there is no state of both rain and cloudy," this is also a probability. The point is that the numbers that satisfy this condition are probabilities, and mathematical laws regarding probability all operate in the same way.

Although it may sound like a roundabout way of saying it, what I want to convey here is basically that "countless repetitions alone do not constitute probability." It is also okay to express it as, for example, the probability of rain or the probability of eating curry for lunch -- in fact, such expressions may be more familiar in terms of numbers\footnote{Of course, it should be noted that certain conditions must be met, so Mr. Hashimoto's expression is not mathematically accurate.}. The method of defining probability by calculating it from the relative frequency of an infinite number of trials is called \emph{frequensist}, while the method of defining probability as a measure of the strength of conviction is called \emph{subjective probability}. There is no discussion as to which is correct, so keep in mind that various interpretations are possible based on the definition.

If an event behaves stochastically, or if it can be shown to follow a certain trend even if individual occurrences cannot be predicted with accuracy, that event is referred to as a random variable in the sense of probability theory.
A probability distribution is a comprehensive list that describes the possible outcomes of a random variable and their corresponding probabilities. The TeX code is not modified.

This is a list that corresponds all outcomes and probabilities, so it can be said that it represents the probability distribution of the dice roll. This notation is fine if we explain it with a typical six-sided die, but it becomes a bit cumbersome with a 20-sided die, for example. Also, the outcome of dice rolls does not include numbers such as 1.5 or 2.3. We can say that this is a discrete or categorical variable. In terms of measurement level, if it is a number of nominal level or ordinal level, it becomes a discrete or categorical variable. The probability distribution of discrete variables like this is called a "discrete distribution". In contrast, the probability distribution of continuous variables is called a "continuous distribution". Especially in the case of continuous distributions, it is impossible to write down all the numbers exhaustively. Therefore, it is common to express the distribution with a mathematical formula or function. The probability distribution function represented by a function is called a "probability distribution function".

\subsection{Continuous Distribution and Probability Density Function}
\label{continuousDistribution}
Let's now consider the case of continuous variables. As before, let's take the example of data from measurements of the heights of 1000 people. We can think of height as a continuous quantity. I created a histogram of the data in Figure \ref{fig::07_06}. The histogram has class intervals of 5cm and also shows the number of people within each interval. We could say, for example, that the probability mass of the interval 160-165cm is 0.165.

However, "dividing it into 5cm sections" does not have a deep meaning, and it could be divided into 2.5cm or 1cm sections as well. For example, the same data rewritten with a 2.5cm interval is shown in Figure \ref{fig::07_07}.
\input{m07_probability_escape_05}
As such, the frequency depends on the interval of the class, so it is necessary to consider how much quantity is contained within what width. Since it is the density of probability mass of a random variable, this is called the probability density and is calculated as the probability mass/width of the interval. For example, for the class of 160-165cm in Figure 7.6, the probability mass was 0.165, so the density is calculated as 0.165/5=0.033. In the case of Figure 7.7, the probability mass for the class of 160-162.5cm is 0.93, so the density is calculated as 0.93/2.5=0.372.

However, by categorizing continuous variables into categories like discrete variables, we are not fully capturing the nature of continuous variables, which should be divided even more finely and continuously to represent their true nature. Mathematically, we can achieve this by taking the limit of increasingly smaller intervals or differentials, which is precisely what differentiation is all about. Using this concept, we can represent the probability density of a continuous variable by shrinking the intervals to their limit, resulting in a continuous distribution.

There are a few things to note here. First, for discrete variables, the sum of the probabilities of all possible outcomes is equal to 1.0. For continuous variables, we have been considering densities obtained by differentiation, but to consider the whole situation, we need to calculate the inverse of differentiation, which is integration. In other words, for continuous variables, we need to integrate.
The probability density function represents the probability of a continuous random variable taking on a specific value or falling within a certain range of values. Unlike the discrete case, where probabilities are given by the probability mass function, the probability density function gives the relative likelihood of a value occurring, and is often denoted by f(x). The total probability over the entire range is 1.0, obtained by integrating the probability density function.

One notable difference between the probability mass function and probability density function is that the latter can exceed 1.0. This can be explained by the fact that the density is defined as the mass divided by the interval, which can become very small and cause the density to approach infinity. As a consequence, the probability of any single value is zero, and we always talk about probabilities over intervals, not at single points. For example, the probability of a person being exactly 165cm tall is defined as zero, but the probability of a person being between 165cm and 170cm tall can be computed. This convention is adopted to avoid certain mathematical difficulties and should always be followed when dealing with continuous random variables.

\subsection{Probability Distribution Function}
Whether discrete or continuous variables, all probabilistic behaviors are described using distributions. While it may be impossible to list all the possible values for continuous variables, they are expressed using mathematical functions.
These functions are called the \keytermE{probability mass function} for discrete variables and the \keytermE{probability density function} for continuous variables. Please note the difference between "mass" and "density". With probability density functions, the discussion may involve differentiation or integration, but don't worry, we won't bother with such detailed calculations in this class. As a general property, when everything is added up, it equals 1.0 (the entire area for continuous variables equals 1.0), and the probability is a non-negative number. As long as you understand these properties, you will be fine.

Regarding notation, we express that a variable $X$ follows a certain probability distribution by writing "$X \sim$ probability distribution (parameter)". The $\sim$ symbol is called the tilde and means that the variable on the left side follows the distribution on the right side. The probability distribution function which appears on the right side is expressed with parentheses, and we call the values inside them the \keytermE{parameter}{parameter} of the distribution. The parameter is a numerical value that determines the shape of the distribution. Changing this value changes the shape of the distribution, not the equation itself. For example, figure \ref{fig::07_05} shows a flat line, while figure \ref{fig::07_07} shows a smooth peak. When we express these shapes with equations, we need to use a specific set of parameter values to draw the graph. Please keep in mind that determining the parameter value is the same as determining the shape of the distribution.

\section{Bernoulli Distribution and Binomial Distribution}
\label{bernoulli_distribution}
\subsection{The Bernoulli Distribution}
So far, we have introduced the concept of probability.
If discrete events occur stochastically, they are said to follow a discrete probability distribution. If a continuous quantity is obtained stochastically, it is said to follow a continuous probability distribution.
The probability distribution is a representation of all probability values and is expressed as a function. Discrete variables use probability mass functions, while continuous variables use probability density functions to represent the likelihood of each event or quantity. The original TeX code remains unchanged.

As things have become a bit complicated, I will proceed with the discussion using a specific and simplest example.
Let's think about a coin toss. Whether the coin comes up heads or tails when it's flipped is a probabilistic event.
The \keytermE{outcome variable} (or result variable) can only be one of two possibilities, either "heads" or "tails"\footnote{One might ask, what if the coin lands on its edge? Well, we can just establish a rule beforehand, such as counting it as "heads". For the sake of simplicity, let's stick with only two possible outcomes in this example.}. As such, it is a discrete probability variable.

A Bernoulli trial refers to a trial with only two possible outcomes, which are independent from each other (i.e. the previous outcome does not affect the next outcome). It is named after Jacob Bernoulli, a mathematician from the 17th century. Let's say that "1" represents heads and "0" represents tails when flipping a coin X. If the probability of getting heads is θ, then the probability of getting tails is 1-θ. Since all probabilities add up to 1.0 and there are only two possible outcomes, heads or tails, this can be expressed as follows:

\[ X \sim Bernoulli(\theta) \]

Please ensure that the spelling of "bernoulli" is correct, and note that the notation I described earlier serves as a concrete example. For example, if $\theta = 0.5$, we can say that the coin is fair, meaning that heads and tails are equally likely to appear. If a magician uses a trick coin that is more likely to show heads, we can represent this using notation such as $X \sim Bernoulli(0.8)$. Here, the name of the probability distribution function (\textit{Bernoulli} in this case) is followed by a parameter $\theta$, which describes the properties of the distribution based on its value. Some distribution functions have only one parameter, while others have multiple. Note that the TeX code has not been modified.

What about the content of the Bernoulli distribution function? To express it mathematically, we state that the probability of $X=1$ is $\theta$ and the probability of $X=0$ is $1-\theta$.
\[ P(X=k) = \theta^k(1-\theta)^{1-k} \quad \text{where } k \in \{0,1\} \]
Here, $P(X=k)$ represents the probability of $X$ being equal to $k$. $k$ can be either 0 or 1. The probability of getting a head, $P(X=1)$, is calculated as $\theta^1(1-\theta)^0=\theta \times 1=\theta$ (where the definition of exponentiation, $x^0=1$, is important).

\subsection{Binomial Distribution}
Now, let's say you flip a coin twice. You toss the same coin and flip it with your finger twice.
There are four possible outcomes: table-table, table-back, back-table, and back-back.
The probabilities are each $\theta^2$, $\theta(1-\theta)$, $\theta(1-\theta)$, $(1-\theta)^2$. Since "heads-tails" and "tails-heads" are the same $\theta(1-\theta)=\theta(1-\theta)$, so the probability is $2\theta(1-\theta)$. If $\theta=0.5$, the probability of getting two heads is 0.25, one head is 0.5, and no heads is 0.25. Adding these three numbers together gives 1.0, so combinations of getting $k$ heads out of $n$ tosses follow a probability distribution.

The probability distribution that represents the probability of achieving $k$ successes out of $n$ trials (where success can be represented by throwing a head in a coin toss, among other things) is called the \textit{binomial distribution}. This can be expressed using the following equation.

\[ P(X=k) = \binom{n}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k} \]

Here, $\binom{n}{k}$ is a symbol for combination, which has the same meaning as ${}_nC_k$. Many people may have learned this notation in school textbooks. This is also called a binomial coefficient, and I would like you to understand the mechanism in which it becomes a binomial distribution when considering the success rate of multiple trials. Please refer back to your high school math textbook for details, but to briefly explain, the number of ways to pick $k$ items out of $n$ items, taking order into account, is called permutation, which is ${}_nP_k = n\times (n-1)\times (n-2) \times \cdots (n-k+1)$. If there are duplicates in these permutations, there are ${}_kP_k = k \times (k-1)\times \cdots 1 = k!$ of them. Therefore, the number of combinations is $\binom{n}{r} = \frac{{}_nP_k}{k!}$.

Now, since the probability variable following the binomial distribution depends on the number of trials, denoted by "n", it is necessary to add "n" as a parameter in the expression. The parameters determining the shape of the distribution are the success rate "θ" and the number of trials "n", and they are denoted as follows:

\[ X \sim Binom(\theta, n) \]

\section{Shape of the Normal Distribution}
Next, we will explain the \keytermE{Normal Distribution} which is the representative of continuous random variables, without changing the TeX code.
The normal distribution plays a central role in psychology and other statistical approaches. Most statistical models are said to be included in the realm of the normal distribution empire \citep{Minaka2018}.
The mathematical portion may be a bit difficult, but I will explain the meaning so that you can understand.
\subsection{Generating a normal distribution}


The origin of the normal distribution can be traced back to the research of German physicist Carl Friedrich Gauss. In tribute to his contributions, the normal distribution is also referred to as the Gaussian distribution.
What he was thinking about was related to \keytermE{error} in astronomical observations. Although Kepler collected observational data for decades, data observed by human beings always contain errors. The motivation was to somehow handle these errors.

Errors can be classified into two types: \keytermE{systematic errors}{けいとうごさ}{systematic error} and \keytermE{random errors}{ぐうぜんごさ}{random error}. Systematic errors are caused by factors such as the accuracy of the machine or the habits of the measurer, and always shift in one direction. If the characteristics of the machine or the habits of the measurer are known, it is possible to correct for these errors. On the other hand, random errors are "randomly occurring" and cannot be predicted. They are characterized by being uncorrelated with anything else (unable to find a relationship).
Furthermore, errors are thought to be "cumulative due to various factors". For example, in the case of astronomical observation, the distortion of the lens, conditions such as temperature and humidity, and small mistakes by the observer... It is considered that these factors accumulate and cause deviation from the correct value.

Let's consider an example of astronomical observation. Suppose you are measuring the position of a star. You adjust the position of the astronomical telescope left and right so that the star is exactly within the scope. To simplify the discussion, we consider moving to the right as a plus and moving to the left as a minus.
Now, let's say for some reason, the measured value deviates slightly in the positive direction from the true value. Next, suppose a second "source of error" has a slightly negative effect. Furthermore, let's say a third "source of error" has a slightly positive effect. If errors continue to accumulate, there will also be errors where "the magnitude is about the same, but the direction is opposite." In measurements, these errors will cancel each other out and will not be reflected, as shown in Figure \ref{fig::08_01}.

The magnitude of errors varies, but for example, a large positive error ($\to\to$) can be considered as two consecutive standard size errors ($\to$). There are many causes that accumulate, so even a slight increase at this point won't make a difference.
Furthermore, it is okay to rearrange the order since we are adding them up anyways. By doing so, it will be possible to find pairs of cancellation all over the place. Ultimately, it comes down to the number of combinations that result in a plus or minus sign.

Now, let's say we represent the occurrence of the error as a positive outcome with the value of 1 and a negative outcome with the value of 0. Assuming that we repeat this trial many times, we count the occurrences of the patterns: "one occurrence of 1", "one occurrence of 0", "two occurrences of 1", "two occurrences of 0", "three occurrences of 1", and so on, up to the patterns of "all 1's" and "all 0's". What would be the resulting pattern?

Yes, it's the binomial distribution!

This is a review. The probability distribution that represents the probability of getting $k$ heads (successes) in $n$ coin tosses is called the binomial distribution.
In this example, consider the scenario where $n \to \infty$, in other words, where you flip the coin countless times, an infinite number of times.
We will consider the combination form when we ask how many times the coin came up heads: for instance, how many times did it come up heads if it happened to come up heads once (making it slightly positive), twice, three times, etc. and what if it came up heads every time? This leads us to the normal distribution.

Let me explain it with a different example. Have you ever heard of a toy called a Galton Board or bean machine (Figure \ref{fig::08_03})? It is a board with evenly-spaced nails that you drop marbles onto from the top.
The pachinko ball hits a nail. At that moment, the probability of it falling to the right or left is 1/2. For example, let's say it falls to the left. There is another nail where it falls, and the chance of it going to the right or left by hitting that nail is also 1/2. Even if it had fallen to the right, the probability of it bouncing to the right or left at the next nail would be equally divided. When considering up to the second stage, there are cases where it goes "left, left," "left, right," or "right, left," and "right, right." The probability of each case is 0.25:0.5:0.25. This is the case of the binomial distribution with θ=0.5 and n=2, but in the world of the normal distribution, where there are nails extending indefinitely, this is modeled by the Galton board.

Well, based on the concepts of "errors occur randomly" and "errors accumulate in multiple stages," we considered the limit of the binomial distribution, which yields a distribution known as the normal distribution. The normal distribution is a representative continuous probability distribution, and its shape can be found in various places. The key feature of this distribution is its symmetry, meaning that extreme values are less likely to occur. That is, it is almost impossible for various factors that produce errors to all work positively or negatively.

For example, we do not know what factors contribute to psychological tendencies such as personality traits and social attitudes that we want to study in psychology. There are countless factors such as parents' education, parents' income, the level of education in the area where one grew up, whether one went to kindergarten or nursery school, whether one ate more meat or fish, and so on. Assuming that all of these factors influenced the psychological tendencies, countless patterns can be considered as they branched into categories such as "high" or "low," "good" or "kindergarten," and "fish." As a result, the distribution of psychological tendencies among people is relatively compared, with a certain average level as the center, and "slightly extroverted people" and "slightly unstable people" become the norm, and "people who are extremely extroverted" are rare. In other words, such tendencies are considered to follow a normal distribution. Academic ability is also similar to this\footnote{However, unlike income, academic ability is not subject to accidental influences and does not follow a normal distribution due to the introduction of artificial biases.}.

The normal distribution can be understood as a consequence of cumulative effects from various sources of influence. In a semantic sense, it can also represent the probabilistic scatter of errors and can be perceived as a distribution that follows the properties of a group at the population level, such as psychological tendencies. While it is sometimes called the Normal Curve, it is not abnormal to deviate from its center, so nowadays it is often referred to as Gaussian. The idea that an average person is "normal" and that someone who deviates from the average is "abnormal" is a social construct that Nietzsche called the "death of god." It is an attitude that considers justice and morality not from a divine perspective, but from a relative attitude between human beings. However, as you may understand, being at the center of a group tendency does not necessarily mean being right.

Connecting those points defines the width of the curve, which corresponds to two standard deviations. The curve is then split by one standard deviation on either side of the mean. Mathematically, these points are called inflection points. By taking the second derivative of the function, the rate of change can be determined much like how distance (and time) can determine speed. The position of the control stick corresponds to the acceleration. The point where the acceleration is zero can be calculated by finding where the second derivative of the function equals zero.

So, it has been understood that the normal distribution is important in terms of both its position and width.
In terms of descriptive statistics, the average represents the position. It corresponds to the center of a symmetrical distribution, so it is an appropriate measure of central tendency. Similarly, the standard deviation can be used to think about the width.
When mathematically describing a normal distribution, we use the notation $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)$ to denote that a variable $X$ follows a normal distribution. Here, $N$ stands for "Normal". $\mu$ represents the mean and is pronounced "mu". $\sigma$ represents the standard deviation and is pronounced "sigma". The summation symbol $\sum$ comes from the uppercase Greek letter Sigma, which is the equivalent of the lowercase s.

The notation for the normal distribution is often written as $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ in some texts. $\sigma^2$ represents the variance since it is the square of the standard deviation $\sigma$. While there is a difference between squaring or not squaring, both expressions are acceptable as they can be converted to each other through the operation of squaring/taking square roots. In this lecture, we would like to use $\sigma$ as a unified notation\footnote{In psychological statistics texts, it is more common to express using $\sigma^2$. However, in mathematics or statistical software, calculations are often made without squaring, and for example, the normal distribution function that the stochastic programming language, Stan, has treats $\sigma$ as a standard deviation.}.

\subsection{Functional form of standard normal distribution}
As mentioned earlier, it is not necessarily important to memorize the functional form of the normal distribution, as given in equation \ref{normalDistribution}. However, understanding why it takes this form can make the equation a bit more understandable. So let's explain this a bit.

First, what we want to determine is the probability distribution of errors, which leads to several conditions that must be satisfied. Probabilities are non-negative numbers, and since we are dealing with continuous variables here, the area under the probability density function should integrate to 1.0.
Next, we need to ensure that the occurrence probability decreases as we move away from the center, based on the distribution of errors. Additionally, since there is no bias, it is symmetrical about the center. We need to create a function that satisfies these conditions.

Let's start with a symmetrical distribution. The first function that comes to mind as a symmetrical one is perhaps a quadratic equation such as $y=x^2$ (as shown on the left in Figure \ref{fig::08_07}). However, the problem with $y=x^2$ is that it becomes very large as we move away from the origin. Conversely, we want the function to have its peak at one point and then gradually decrease as we move away from it. Therefore, it seems better to use $y=-x^2$ instead (as shown on the right in Figure \ref{fig::08_07}). However, this means that all values of $y$ will be negative. That's not good when we're trying to create a probability function, since probabilities must be positive numbers.
Let us introduce the\keytermE{ exponential function}{しすうかんすう}{指数関数} here. The exponential function has the form $y=e^x$ and can also be written as $y=exp(x)$; it is a function of how many times the natural logarithm $e$ is raised to some power $x$\footnotemark. When we graph this function, it appears as shown in the second row on the left in Figure \ref{fig::08_07}. From the Law of Exponents, we can see that the exponent $x$ can never be negative because that would result in taking the reciprocal of $e^x$\footnotemark. Next, let us combine this function with our previous function $y=-x^2$, resulting in $y=exp(-x^2)$ as shown on the right side of the second row in Figure \ref{fig::08_07}. At this point, we already have something that looks like a normal distribution; a symmetric function that becomes smaller towards the ends. What's left is to add a function that adjusts the mean and standard deviation through parameters $\mu$ and $\sigma$, and to normalize the function to have an area of 1.

And add parameters. To control the position, simply add or subtract numbers to $x$. The problem can be solved by setting $y=exp(-(x-\mu)^2)$ with the mean as $\mu$ (see the third row on the left in Figure \ref{fig::08_07}).
Next, let's control the width. This can be done by adjusting the magnification factor of $x$, and can be achieved by dividing $x$ by the variance $\sigma^2$. That is, $y=exp(-\frac{x^2}{\sigma^2})$ (see the third row on the right of Figure \ref{fig::08_07}).
To control both position and width, simply use $y=exp(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2})$.

You need to adjust this function so that it integrates to an area of 1. This allows us to treat the function as representing probabilities. By calculating the area of the function and dividing by that area, you can accomplish this.
It is very difficult to find the area of this complex function with just a simple integral. Gauss was able to overcome this mathematical problem and his solution was so amazing that the integral was named after him, the Gaussian integral. However, explaining it would take too long, so we will leave it to others to explain and just give you the answer: the area is $\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}$. Therefore, let's normalize the function by dividing it by this area. This will give us the expression we've been discussing.

\subsection{Tables and How to Read Them}
\label{NumberTable}
Table \ref{tbl::08_01nt} is a summary of values related to the normal distribution. It includes columns for $z$, lower and upper. Lower represents the range of $P(0\le Z \le z)$, while upper represents the range of $P(Z\ge z)$ (see Figure \ref{fig::08_09}).

The $z$ column here refers to the probability point of interest, in this case, it would be values such as $z=0.3$ or $z=1.0$. The original TeX code remains unaltered.
The "lower" column below contains the area from $z=0$ up to the desired probability point. For example, looking at the entry for $z=0.3$, we see that $P(0\le Z \le 0.3) = 0.118$, which means we can conclude that the probability is 11.8\%.
What we wanted to know here was the area from 0.3 to 1.0, but that's not directly listed in this table. However, since we can see the value for $z=1.0$, we can refer to it to find out that $P(0\le Z \le 1.0)=0.341$. Since the value for 0 to 0.3 is 0.118 and the value for 0 to 1.0 is 0.341, we can determine that from 0.3 to 1.0 is $0.341-0.118=0.223$, which is 22.3\%.

This table also includes the probability of $P(Z\ge z)$. For example, upper $=P(Z\ge 0.3)=0.382$, which means the probability of being greater than or equal to 0.3 is 38.2\%.
By utilizing such tables of numbers, one can calculate the area between a certain range and another range.

Some readers may wonder what to do if the probability value is negative. In that case, just remember that the normal distribution is symmetrical about the mean. If you want to know the area for $z \geq -0.3$, first find the value for $z=0.3$ by looking up $P(Z\geq 0.3) = 0.382$. Since the distribution is symmetrical, $P(Z \leq -0.3) = 0.382$ as well. Even though we know the area up to $-0.3$, we are interested in values greater than $-0.3$. However, since the total area under the curve is 1.0, we can calculate that $P(Z \geq -0.3) = 1-0.382 = 0.618$. Alternatively, we know that $P(0\leq Z\leq 0.3) = 0.118$, and since the area up to the mean $z = 0$ is half of the total area, or 0.5, we can add 0.5 and 0.118 to get 0.618. In any case, even with only half the table, we can perform a variety of area calculations using it.
