Here, psychology is divided into two approaches: empirical approach and interpretive approach. It is also commonly referred to as fundamental and applied, but the former division is more appropriate in terms of content. The main fields include fundamental psychology and clinical psychology, but within fundamental psychology, there are further academic areas such as perception, physiology, learning, and cognition.

\section{Characteristics of Modern Science}

If psychology is a science, what exactly is the science of psychology? When we consider the history of the West, we see that scholarship originated from Greek philosophy, and until the Middle Ages, Christianity provided a dominant framework for explaining the world. In order for modern science to emerge, we needed the Renaissance, which was led by figures such as Newton and Galileo. So what exactly was the transformation in thinking during that time?

The features of modern science can be summarized into four points. The names of the people associated with each idea are also listed below.
\input{m01_introduction_escape_03}

First of all, being empiricist means a rebellion against the idea of "we don't need to check it because it's written in the textbook." It's a style of "let's check if it's true or not." In the West, what was written in the Bible was considered the answer to the world's questions for a long time. The Bible said that heavy objects fall faster than light objects. It's because God made it that way. If you doubt this, you're doubting God, and you'll go to hell, they say. However, Galileo tried to verify this by experimenting. He dropped heavy and light objects from the Leaning Tower of Pisa at the same time and confirmed that they landed at the same time. It's not enough to discuss it speculatively, we have to actually try and verify it. Other astronomers, such as Tycho Brahe and Kepler, recorded the movements of celestial bodies for many years and considered what laws governed them. In terms of taking actual data and considering it, this is also an empirical attitude, and this is the basic style of science.

Quantitativism or mathematical phenomenology is the idea of describing phenomena using mathematics. It is often said that Newton discovered gravity, but in reality he expressed how force acts using equations rather than simply observing gravity. The goal is to gain a deeper understanding of the world by taking data and describing the relationships between them\footnote{This research was carried out not by doubting or rebelling against the existence of God, but rather from a belief that "God designed nature so perfectly that it is amazing", as recommended by \citet{IkeuchiR2014} and \citet{Hebizo2016} for more details.}. The crucial point is that there was a fundamental shift in questioning from "What" gravity is to "How" it works, and this laid the foundation for modern science.

The mechanical way of thinking is the idea that "the world is a machine, with mechanisms and procedures." For a long time in the West, it was believed that the world was created by God, and if you tried to explore its workings, you would be punished. However, Descartes believed that "God created the mind, but the body can be dissected and studied to learn about God's ideas."
The purpose of science is to explore this mechanism!

The idea of element reductionism is to break things down to their fundamental elements. By breaking down large problems into smaller elements and assembling the gained knowledge along the way, eventually the entire large problem can be understood. This approach is also based on Descartes' philosophy, which can be read about in his book, "Discourse on Method" \citep{Descartes}. Breaking down big problems into smaller ones is also a fundamental approach in modern scientific methodology.

Now, psychology is a science, so it inherits the four basic attitudes listed here unchanged. Also, please note that the foundation of these four characteristics lies in the exclusion of privacy.
The term "privacy" may not be familiar to you, but it essentially refers to the quality of "a secret that only I know." A scientific approach aims to eliminate this characteristic thoroughly.
I, as an individual, of course, as well as specific organizations such as a church, and even the omniscient and omnipotent God, are included.
Eliminating any incomprehensible parts and thoroughly verbalizing, explaining the world in a way that can be understood by anyone is the ultimate purpose of science\footnote{In this sense, science is essentially communication.}. Without communication, science cannot fully achieve its objective.

Therefore, psychology naturally takes an "experimental," "quantitative," "mechanistic," and "elemental reductionist" approach. By subdividing the problem and actually conducting experiments or interventions, the mechanism is clarified. However, some people may wonder why we use numbers! Please do not misunderstand, numbers are "words." It is not ordinary language, but in advancing scientific pursuits, it is superior to ordinary language in the following ways.

\section{Subtleties of Psychology}

As mentioned above, using numerical (mathematical) terms to express phenomena has clear advantages, such as being precise and verifiable, as well as summarizing a vast amount of information. However, some may still say, "No, there are things that cannot be expressed through numbers. That is what the heart is for."

Sure, although the subject of psychology is "the mind," we acknowledge that it is invisible and intangible. Furthermore, one might even question its existence. Some may argue, "Of course there is a mind!" However, in accordance with the principles of modern science, we must express this idea with caution. To say "I know whether the mind exists or not" is an inappropriate argument (as it requires exclusion of subjectivity on my part). Please consider this.

Psychologists have traditionally approached this problem by adopting the assumption, "Let's assume that there is something, although we don't know for sure." If there is such a thing as the mind, then any external stimulus should evoke some kind of response through the mind. For example, if someone gets punched (stimulus), they will start crying (response). This is because the punch made them sad, and that is the function of the mind. In psychology, to think scientifically, various stimuli are given to the subject (not just humans, but anything with a mind) and by observing their response patterns, it might be said that "they have a mind". This is similar to Newton's reasoning of whether there is "gravity" based on the relationship between an apple falling and something else. He expressed this relationship mathematically (mathematical phenomenology). "Mind" can also be described as a way of explaining the cause-effect relationship of "if we do this, the mind causes this to happen".

By abstracting and expressing specific phenomena, as seen with the figure in the previous example (Figure 1.1), we are able to improve our understanding and come up with new interpretations. The ideal representation of this abstraction is called a \textit{model}. In psychology, the subject's mind is assumed to be a model, and if it corresponds to reality, then we can consider that in response to certain stimuli as predicted by theory, certain reactions would appear. Thus, various stimuli are considered, various reactions are observed, with the aim of verifying the correctness of the model known as the mind.

Of course, there are various patterns of stimuli that can be considered. Countless have been proposed, and there are likely to be various types of reactions in response to each one. Even for a single stimulus, there are individual differences, so there should be various variations of responses. To accurately record these multiple stimuli and responses, quantification is necessary. Quantification enables summarization, and by expressing the relationship between these stimuli and responses using mathematical formulas, it is possible to generalize the findings. By substituting actual numerical values into these formulas, it is possible to verify whether the findings fit the facts. With this in mind, psychology cannot avoid using numbers. Indeed, using numbers and quantification is essential in other academic fields as well. In pure math areas such as physics, it is necessary, and in engineering, it analyzes measured data and considers correspondence with physical theories, which is called a scientific academic field. Nonetheless, it is not unrealistic to use numbers in humanities and social sciences.

Statistics is not just about simple aggregation. Especially in "psychological" statistics, it is crucially important to determine how to measure and collect data in order to see the invisible aspects of the mind. These are called \keytermE{psychometric} methods and are considered in various fields of psychology. However, once numerical data is obtained, there are various methods across domains for interpreting mechanisms and building models. This is what you should learn in this course, Psychological Data Analysis. You will go on to various specialized fields, but you need to start from the very basics of psychology to accumulate common knowledge and wisdom about data analysis.

Some people might feel that they don't understand things that are presented in numerical form, but it could be due to issues with psychological measurement and how the numbers were chosen. For example, someone may be happy or sad, but to say that happiness is 4 or sadness is 23 might not make sense to them because they don't know who decided those values. Even if the numbers accurately represented one's emotional state, it would be difficult to summarize a large dataset or account for individual differences to obtain a true picture of someone's mental state. Recently, there are opportunities to record people's behaviors in many different ways, leading to the concept of "big data." Analyzing people's behavior patterns from this data falls under the realm of psychological statistics. Applications of psychological statistics include criminal profiling and marketing research. Additionally, psychological statistics can evaluate the effects of clinical interventions or experimental manipulations. By quantifying things, we can better understand the degree of our uncertainty surrounding them and the extent to which we can comprehend them.
